# CLAUDE.md

灯 Tomoshibi (トモシビ) — a web app that generates **3D-printable forming molds (harigata)** for building washi paper lanterns (paper lamps). Editing the cross-section directly produces STLs for the ribs, koma (hubs), and stand.

## Terminology and roles (read this first)

The real-world lantern-making process: **wind bamboo ribs onto the mold → paste on washi paper → once dry, disassemble the mold and pull it out**. This app is about 3D-printing that mold as separable parts.

> **Grooves are horizontal rings by default** (identical position across every rib). An optional **spiral winding** mode (`p.spiral`) offsets the grooves per rib (`step/boards` each) so the bamboo forms one continuous descending helix; because each rib is then a different shape, spiral molds export one STL per rib, each engraved with its serial number (7-segment through-cut). Each part's role is as follows (**these definitions and relationships are fixed — do not change them arbitrarily in the implementation or explanations**).

```
        ● koma (top)         ← gear-like hub that gathers and bundles the rib tabs
      ┌──┐  neck (top)       ← vertical rectangle whose only job is positioning the opening (optional)
      │  │
     ╱    ╲                  lamp body (hibukuro) = the lantern's body shape.
    │ groove→ │ ← bamboo rib  Surface of revolution the washi is pasted onto. Bamboo-rib grooves on the outer edge.
    │ groove→ │
     ╲    ╱
      │  │  neck (bottom)     ← top and bottom are independent (neckBot / neckTop)
      └──┘
        ● koma (bottom)
       ╱ ╲                   ↑ tab (tsume) = the straight tongue at the rib's end. Plugs into the koma.
      stand (2 posts + base)  Receives the koma in a U-shaped saddle, holding it clear of the floor.
```

| Term | Role / definition |
|---|---|
| **lamp body (hibukuro / 火袋)** | The lantern's **body shape**. The curved surface the washi is pasted onto. The curved portion **between** the outermost control points of the cross-section profile. Bamboo-rib grooves go here and only here. |
| **rib (haneita / 羽根板)** | The **radial plate** that forms the mold's surface (N of them, arranged like the segments of a mandarin orange). A flat plate printed lying flat. Its outer edge is the lamp-body curve (+ grooves), with a tab at each end. It assembles by plugging into the two koma (top and bottom). Its inner edge is a curve **hollowed inward only at the center** (`ribInnerX()`) — to make it easy to pull out through the opening after drying (the same intent as carving the inside of a real forming mold into a half-moon). Note: "rib" (haneita) is a coined term for this app. Real craftspeople don't name the individual parts; they count by plate count — "**6-plate, 8-plate, 10-plate mold**". |
| **bamboo rib (higo / 竹ひご)** | The thin bamboo **wound horizontally** around the outside of the lamp body. The lantern's horizontal rings. It seats along the grooves on the rib's outer edge. Also called "**higo / bamboo bone**" in the real craft. |
| **groove (mizo / bamboo-rib groove)** | The **V-notch** carved into the rib's outer edge. So the bamboo rib seats in and doesn't slip off, the barb (tooth tip) leans toward **the center = the equator**. **Evenly spaced.** None are cut at the neck (near the opening). In craftspeople's terminology this side groove on the forming mold is called "**higo-me (ひご目)**" (the finer the count, the finer the work). |
| **opening (kaikou / 開口)** | The **mouth** at the top and bottom of the lamp body. = the radius of the outermost control point. It is the edge of the washi and the position where the koma/tabs connect. In real work this rim corresponds to the "**opening ring (kuchiwa / 口輪)**". |
| **neck (kubi / neck)** | The **vertical rectangular collar** standing outside the opening. Its role is **"only" to set the position and size of the opening**. It is independent top/bottom and can be present or absent (`neckBot`/`neckTop`). **Always a rectangle — never angled.** **The presence of a neck does not change the tab size.** Without a neck, the opening becomes the tab size. The neck carries no bamboo rib, no groove, and no washi. |
| **tab (tsume / tab)** | The **straight tongue** at the rib's end. It plugs into the koma's notch. Width = **board thickness `boardT`** (nominally equal to the koma's notch width). Only the notch side adds the **print tolerance `fit`** to become `boardT + fit`, leaving a real-world fit clearance (the tab itself stays `boardT`). No **outward** steps, hooks, or protrusions. **Both tabs** have an **L-notch dented into the tip's inner corner** (`TAB_DENT_W`×`TAB_DENT_H`), which the koma's shallower notch mates with: the wider tab base then catches the koma's solid hub and stops it sliding inward. In a lantern maker's mold there is no neck and "tab = neck" (which is why the neck is an optional design element). |
| **koma (コマ)** | The **small gear-like hub** that gathers and bundles the tabs (two of them, top and bottom, completely identical). Notches around the rim where the tabs plug in. Outer diameter = `komaR`. The stand receives it. This is a **real craft term**; in the trade it is also called "**kagami (mirror / 鏡)**" (the forming mold is assembled and fixed by inserting it into the grooves of the top and bottom koma = kagami). |
| **stand (dodai / 土台)** | The **base** that supports the assembled mold. Two **posts** of constant thickness receive the koma's rim in a U-shaped saddle, and a single base plate holds the posts at the correct spacing. Fully flat, needs no print supports. Users often reuse it (they don't print it every time). |
| **opening ring (kuchiwa / 口輪)** | **The one exported part that is not part of the mold** — it stays in the finished lantern, which has no koma left in it once the mold is pulled out. A thin flat hoop (`ringGeometry`, `RING_WALL`/`RING_H` = 2mm) glued around the opening to hold it round, sized from `openingR()` so it follows the design like every other part. The **bottom** one also carries the **leg sockets** (see below); the top one is a plain hoop. |
| **leg socket (脚ソケット)** | The **flat "onigiri" pads** inside the bottom opening ring — `LEG_N`=3 of them at 120°, each pointing its rounded vertex at the centre with a **bore** (`LEG_D`=6mm) at its middle that a leg rod pushes into. This is what makes the bottom ring double as the base of a **stand for the finished lantern**. Each pad's two outer corners stay sharp and overlap the hoop's inner rim by `LEG_OVERLAP`, so the whole thing prints as one piece; the pads are the same height as the hoop (flat, no supports). Presence is `p.legSockets` — **a checkbox, not dimensions** (see below). |

**Assembly relationships**: rib (tab) → plugs into → **koma** → sits on → **stand**. (The **opening ring** joins nothing here — it belongs to the finished lantern, after the mold is taken apart.) The bamboo rib winds into the **grooves** on the rib's outer edge. The rib's outer edge revolved forms the **lamp body** surface onto which the washi is pasted. The **neck** positions the opening (a vertical rectangle) and is **independent of the tab size**.

## Commands

```bash
npm run dev       # Vite dev server (HMR, --host). http://localhost:8173/ (strictPort: a clash fails loudly)
npm run build     # Production build (= tsc --noEmit && vite build). Always confirm it passes after changes.
npm run typecheck # Just the type check (what `build` runs first). The whole repo: src, scripts, vite.config.
npm run preview   # Preview the build output
```

No test runner. **Correctness is verified by "the build passes" + "the STL is watertight (manifold)"** (see below).

## Tech stack

Vite 8 + React 19 + three.js r185 + fflate, in **TypeScript** (`.ts` / `.tsx` throughout, `strict`). Minimal dependencies — four runtime deps; the dev ones are the toolchain plus the type packages (`typescript`, `typescript-eslint`, `@types/react`, `@types/react-dom`, `@types/three`, `@types/node`), which ship nothing.

Three consequences of the TypeScript setup worth knowing before you edit anything:

- **Relative imports carry their real extension** — `./geometry.ts`, `./ui/controls.tsx`. Vite would resolve them without one, but the five check scripts are run by plain `node`, which strips types (Node ≥ 22.18) and does **not** map a `.js` specifier onto a `.ts` file. One set of source files, two runners, so the extension is written out. `allowImportingTsExtensions` in `tsconfig.json` is what permits it, and `noEmit` is its precondition: nothing here is ever emitted by `tsc`.
- **Node ≥ 22.18 is now the floor** (`engines`, and the CI matrix), for that same reason. Node 20 reached end of life in April 2026; the old floor was never a choice, only where Vite 8 and ESLint 10 stopped working.
- **The check scripts are `.mts` and are type-checked with everything else.** They are the safety net this project is built on, so an untyped island there would be the weakest link. A corollary: **nothing a check script imports may be a `.tsx` file** — Node strips types but cannot compile JSX. In practice that is already true (they import geometry, config, papercraft, pdf, i18n), and it is the one rule the compiler cannot state for you.

## Architecture

- **`src/geometry.ts`** — the core, and the **single import point for every shape**. **Pure functions** that generate the cross-section profile and the 2D cross-sections / 3D geometry of every part. They return three.js Shape/ExtrudeGeometry but **have no dependency on React or the DOM** (shared by section drawing, 3D preview, STL export and both paper templates — which is why what you see is what you print). The file itself is now a **re-export barrel**; the implementation sits in `src/geometry/`, one module per part:
  - **`geometry/profile.ts`** — `outerR()` and **every dimension derived from it** (`komaR`, `tabDepth`, `innerRi`, `notchR`, `maxBoards`). These share a module because they are genuinely mutually recursive, not merely related: `outerR` calls `komaR` (a neck-less end *is* the tab size) and `komaR` calls `outerR` via `bodyMinR`'s self-intersection guard. **The print-fit invariants live here** (see "Part joints" below) — this is the file to be careful in.
  - **`geometry/groove.ts`** — groove positions / width / the grooved outer edge · **`geometry/shape.ts`** — point list → `THREE.Shape` with the cleanup earcut requires · **`geometry/rib.ts`** · **`geometry/koma.ts`** · **`geometry/ring.ts`** (the opening rings + the bottom one's leg sockets) · **`geometry/stand.ts`** · **`geometry/washi.ts`**
  - **Import from `geometry.ts`, not from the modules.** The barrel's public surface is deliberately the same 40 names it was before the split, so moving a function between modules stays a non-event for callers. Dependencies run one way (profile ← groove ← rib; shape is a leaf) — **anything mutually recursive with `outerR` belongs in `profile.ts` by construction**, or you have created a cycle.
  - Add geometry logic to the module that owns the part. `check:hash` makes any move provably shape-neutral: the split itself was verified at zero vertex diff across all 1152 parts.
- **`src/types.ts`** — **what a design IS**: `Design` (the `p` passed to nearly every function), `Pt` / `Handle` (the control points), `Pt2` (an `[x, y]` outline point), `Route`, and `NumericDesignKey`. Types only, so every import of it is `import type` and erased — which is what lets `geometry/` name a design without importing `config.ts`, a dependency it deliberately does not have. `DEFAULTS` is now checked against `Design`, and `SIL_ROWS` and persist's `BOUNDS` are both keyed by `NumericDesignKey`, so a numeric field added to the design without a range to clamp it into fails the build instead of reaching `outerR` unclamped from a corrupt file.
- **`src/config.ts`** — `PRESETS` (control-point templates for shapes) / `DEFAULTS` (initial parameters) / `SIL_ROWS` (scrub-row definitions) / **`LIMITS`** (how far the body may be stretched — see "How big may a design get" below).
- **`src/SectionEditor.tsx`** — the **direct-manipulation editor** for the cross-section (SVG). Drag/add/delete control-point ◇ handles, toggle corner/smooth, and visualize the neck/lamp-body/tab (rib). It uses `geometry.ts`'s `outerR` etc. directly so it matches the 3D/STL exactly.
- **`src/TomoshibiStudio.tsx`** — the app's **state and composition only**. It holds the design `p` and the transient UI state, wires the hooks, and lays out the viewport + inspector. It renders no control of its own and builds no 3D — those moved out (below). Keep it that way: the moment a scene detail or a button's styling lands back here, the file starts growing towards the 1,400 lines it used to be.
- **`src/three/viewport.ts`** — everything created **once per mount**: renderer, post-processing chain, lights, shared materials, camera-orbit input, and the render loop. Input is a single **pointer-event** path (mouse / touch / pen, with pinch derived from the live pointers) and every listener is removed on dispose — the earlier mouse+touch pair left window listeners behind, so a StrictMode remount rotated the camera twice per drag.
- **`src/three/scenes.ts`** — `buildScene()` and one builder per view (`mold` / `print` / `lit`). Two views draw no 3D at all and say so by returning early: `2d`, and `print` on the cardboard route — both are documents drawn over this canvas instead. It empties the viewport's group and refills it. Every shape comes from `geometry.ts`; the only geometry it touches is rotating parts flat for the **print preview**, which is preview-only (the export lays parts out itself).
- **`src/bed.ts`** — `fitOnBed()`. Shared by the overflow warning, the recommended max height, and the plate layout, so those three can never disagree.
- **`src/hooks.ts`** — `useUndoRedo` (watches `p` and commits once it settles, since there is no single choke point for edits) / `useAutosave` / `useNarrow` (`useSyncExternalStore`, so the first render already knows) / `useLang`.
- **`src/ui/`** — `theme.ts` (the palette + the `t` context — see below), `controls.tsx` (ScrubRow / Stepper / NumInput / Checkbox / SegButton / CTA / Note — ScrubRow takes an optional `curve` for rows whose range far outruns the sizes people work at), and the panel's larger pieces: `PresetChips.tsx`, `PointCard.tsx`, `Toolbar.tsx` (undo/redo only — see below), `Menu.tsx` (the header's `☰` menu), `PointBar.tsx` (the phone's contextual bar for the selected ◇) and `pointEdit.ts` (the point-editing rules those last two share).
- **`src/GuidePage.tsx`** — the **build guide** (作り方): how to build the lantern. It is a document, so it takes the whole window and scrolls, and it is an **overlay** — opened from the `☰` menu, closed with × or Esc, the same shape the welcome card has. **It was a fifth view tab and should not go back.** The other four views are each a rendering of YOUR design (move a ◇ and all four redraw); this one is not, and the app kept having to say so one gate at a time — the dimension chip excepted out (`view !== "guide"`), the viewport alerts excepted out (an alert reading 「断面図で開口を広げる」 over a page with no section editor on it), the inspector excepted out through a `solo` flag that existed for this and lit alone, and on a phone a fifth tab that pushed the strip into the dimension chip. As an overlay it needs none of them: `solo` is gone, both gates are back to plain `isLit`, and the tab strip has its fifth slot back. **The name is 作り方, not 組み立て方**, and the page's own kicker says the same word (it used to read 「組立説明書」): winding bamboo, pasting washi, drying, pulling the mold out and lighting the result are not assembly, and half of them happen after the mold comes apart. 「組立」 is also the tab immediately to the left. **The page is generic: every figure is drawn from one fixed design (`GUIDE_P` = `DEFAULTS` **with the leg sockets pinned on**, plus a `paperP` copy for the cardboard route), and no dimension is printed anywhere on it.** It used to be the opposite — the parts measured off `geometry.ts` and every figure rebuilt when a slider moved — and that was dropped at the user's request: two dozen WebGL scenes on every edit, bought with numbers nobody needs, because winding bamboo onto a mold and pasting paper over it is the same job at ⌀140 as at ⌀400. Figures are therefore built at most **once per route per session** (`CACHE` in GuidePage) and a return visit costs no WebGL at all. **What this gives up is the old guarantee that the page could not describe a mold the download does not contain, so nothing on it may state a QUANTITY the design decides**: the rib's line reads 「設計した枚数」 rather than ×8 for exactly that reason (a fixed picture of eight ribs is an illustration; a printed 8 is a claim), while ×2 koma, ×2 posts, ×1 base and ×1 ring each stay, being facts about the mold. The one thing that still follows the app is the **route**, because that changes which parts exist at all, not how big they are. Between that list and the steps sits **`KIT`** — what you supply yourself (bamboo, washi, paste, tape-or-thread, a razor, a lamp, wire; two brushes, a mister and pliers). **Plain strings, no numbers, nothing derived**: a wire gauge and a brush are not things the design decides, and a bamboo length summed over the grooves was tried and taken straight back out — arithmetic nobody asked for on a list whose job is to be read in a shop. Its items carry `opt` (a 「任意」 badge on the name line, where the parts list puts its ×N — the same line answering the same question) and each one draws, in the parts grid's own card and well, from a `fig` in figures.ts (see the kit note there). What is optional is a judgement, not a category: the wire and the pliers serve only the two lighting ways with wire in them (way (1) stands a lamp on the floor and needs neither), and the brushes and the mister are things a finished lantern can be got to without, so **everything else is unconditional** — without bamboo, washi, paste, something to hold the bamboo while it dries, a blade to trim the paper and a lamp to put inside, there is no lantern at the end. The **razor sits among the materials rather than the tools** because that is where it was asked for; it is also the one cutting thing on the page you use up. Tape and thread are **one card and one drawing** (`kitStick`), and the card is named for the job rather than for either of them (「テープや糸など」): anything that holds the bamboo while the paste dries will do, a card each would be the list saying the same thing twice, and a note hung under one of them ("or superglue") reads as an alternative to that one rather than to both. **Every step is drawn** — winding the bamboo, pasting the washi, drying, pulling the mold, and the lantern lit. A step may also carry a **`more`** block: the body says what to do, and this says how it goes, over a **photograph** from `public/photos/` (and, optionally, a short clip over that still). **Two steps carry one — winding and pasting** — and so do **two of the light step's three ways**, the two that bend wire (`hang`, `legs`); an option's block sits after its numbered work and above its asterisked `note`, which stays last whatever precedes it. The step list was cut to those two from four: drying is waiting and pulling the mold is one motion the figure already shows, and way (1) sets a lamp down and drops the shade over it, so a block on any of them would have been a well opened because the mechanism existed rather than because there was more to say. A `more` is for work whose difficulty is in the HAND, where a drawing cannot say how hard to press or how wet the paste is — not for everything that could hold one. **Only winding's is written**; the other three keep an empty well and 「未記入」, which is the same admission the light step's unfilled sub-slots used to make, and the reason the file it wants is NAMED there: the layout finishes before the photography does, and a picture lands by being dropped in under that name without GuidePage being touched. A `video` needs its `photo` — the still is what prints, what shows before the clip loads, and what is left if the clip is never shot, so nothing about the method may live only in the video. **It is folded (`<details>`), never a modal**: a closed modal's content is not in the page, so it does not print, and printing is the only paper version this page has. The reader at a bench with paste on their hands is exactly the one who wants the detail and exactly the one who cannot click for it. Print styles open every block and drop the summary — via `::details-content`, because Chrome hides a closed `<details>` with `content-visibility` that no rule on the element itself reaches; without that one line the whole mechanism is invisible on paper, which is where it matters most. Why the drawn ones are drawn: the rings come from the same `grooveList`/`higoSpiralPath` that cut the grooves (so a spiral mold is drawn as one continuous helix), and the panels are one lathe per bay over `fukuroRange` at `outerR + higoD` — the lit view's own skin radius — so the seams fall on the ribs of the mold being drawn. Neither is something a photograph of somebody else's lantern can say, and both are why these stayed drawings when they stopped being derived. The washi figure is the mold **in its stand** (upright on cardboard, which has no stand) with panels on **every other bay** — the order the step asks for — and two fixed points behind it: **pasted panels are ivory**, breaking the white-parts rule, because paper laid over a white part on a white card is invisible; and the **mold is yawed so a bay centre faces the camera**, which is the only way two alternating panels are both fully front-facing (a bay is 360/N wide, so any other phase lays one across the silhouette, where its inside shows through the bare bay with the bamboo drawn over it). A mold has no preferred rotation, so the yaw costs nothing. The **paper's surface is the mold's offset along its NORMAL**, not along x (`washiProfile`): a horizontal offset leaves only `higoD·cos θ` of clearance on a face at angle θ, and past ~60° the bamboo comes out through the paper it is under — a squat body (r=120 on h=90 is dR/dy = 2) drew its bamboo as rings printed on the outside of the shade. The **pull** figure is the same dry shade **lying on its side**, both koma set down flat and the **last** rib half drawn out of the opening (one rib, not eight: they come out one at a time, and with the rest still in, the mouth fills with rib edges seen end-on and reads as a turbine): the rib leaves along the axis, so upright the whole action points at the camera. It is the rib that faces the camera (`faceOnRib`), brought onto the axis on its way out — which is what the hollowed inner edge is for — and it leaves by the **wider of the two openings**: a rib is as wide as the body is deep, and the default lantern's mouths are ⌀148 and ⌀38, so out of the small one it does not read as the wrong end, it reads as the rib tearing through the paper. The bands are gone, because with the koma off there is nothing for them to hold; the mold is yawed 15° off square (`PULL_YAW`) because at 0° the mouth faces the reader and the frame fills with the inside of the lantern, and at 45° it is edge-on and the shade flattens into a leaf. The **light** step is the finished lantern with no mold in it at all — warm rather than white, because a lit shade is the same paper with light behind it, and no rays (a starburst is the one mark on a page of technical drawings that would be decoration). It is the one step with **`options`**: three ways of supplying the lamp, each its own **section** with its own figure. **Sections, not steps** — numbering them 11/12/13 would tell the reader to do all three — and each needs its own drawing, because the difference between the three *is* the picture. Each way's **title and body sit above its figure**, spanning both columns — that is what separates one from the next. Beside the figure the title is only the first line of a paragraph in a column of paragraphs. It also leaves the right-hand column holding nothing but the numbered work, so **that column's first well starts level with the main drawing** rather than a paragraph's depth below it. (Giving each way its own card was tried and taken back out: it separated them, but by more than they needed.) (1) `lightSet`, a lamp stood on the floor and the shade dropped over it, drawn **exploded**: set down it is indistinguishable from the other two with their fittings cropped off, and lifted it states the whole method. The floor disc is what separates "stood on the floor" from "floating", and the lamp is generic (a base and a dome, no thread) sized off the **bottom opening it has to pass through**. (2) `lightHang`, hung from a pendant cord: the cord is on the **axis** and enters by the **top** opening whichever is wider — a hanging shade has an up, and it is the design's own up, not its bigger mouth — and its top is simply **cut off**, the convention for "this continues"; a ceiling rose would claim the lamp is wired in when it plugs into a socket. It draws the **hanger** as well — the one wire hanging inside the opening with the cord in the U bent into its middle — because what carries the shade is not the lamp: the lamp hangs on the cord inside it, and a figure without that wire is a shade hanging on nothing. (3) `lightLegs`, stood on legs with the lamp fixed up into the bottom mouth: the legs are the lit view's (`scenes.ts` buildLit: 0.42·height drop, 0.35·drop splay) rooted in the bottom ring's **sockets** (`ringLegs` gives the pad centres). Its cord **turns and runs out of the frame** — straight down between three legs it read as a fourth leg — and **this is the one figure on the page whose shade is see-through**. Everywhere else paper is drawn as paper, opaque, and what is behind it is hidden; here the entire fitting the three panels beside it build — socket, bulb, three legs and the frame — is INSIDE the lantern, and drawn opaque the figure showed a shade with legs under it and explained none of it. So the skin drops to `LIT_THRU` and the whole assembly is drawn in, in the same accent the panels use, which is also what a lit paper shade looks like. (It used to draw no socket at all, for the opposite reason: anything in the bottom mouth was behind the paper. That reasoning went with the opacity.) **An option can carry `detail`** — the numbered work inside it, numbered *because* it is sequential, which is exactly what the options around it are not. A slot is filled in by gaining `title`/`body`/`fig`; without a `fig` it keeps an empty well and 「未記入」, which says the step is unfinished where three tidy sentences would say it was finished and thin. **(3) carries four and (2) carries two**, and neither way needs a printed part — wire is all either of them adds. (3): the socket's cord leaves through a threaded stem with a fixing nut on it, so a loop bent in the end of each wire stacks on that stem and one nut clamps the lot (`legBend`/`frameBend`/`legStack`/`legStood` in figures.ts). **Four wires, not three**: besides the legs there is the **frame (枠)** — a tall closed hoop standing inside the shade, an eye at its foot on the same stem and a small loop at its peak that shows at the top opening. A paper bag with a ring at each end and nothing between them sags shut, and that is what the frame is for; it is bent from the same wire and held by the same nut, which is why it is a step of this way rather than a way of its own. **The nut is its only fixing** — it presses nothing at the bottom, and holding the top out against a foot that cannot move is what puts the shade in tension. That is also what shapes it: its foot sits directly under the socket, which sits directly under the bulb, so it leaves the eye upward AND outward and is at full width by a fifth of its height. Clearing the lamp is the one real constraint on this wire; a hoop with the flat bottom bar and straight sides a photograph of one shows passes both by millimetres, which is clearing them by accident. Its size is stated as a RELATION and never as a number — follows the inside of the body, stands a little proud of the top opening — because it follows the shade the reader built and this page knows nothing about that shade. (2): **one wire**, bowed into a shallow **arch** over the top opening — highest in the middle, its two ends running straight on down through the rim and out past the ring, UNDER it — with a **U** bent into its middle whose gap passes the CORD and stops the SOCKET (`hangBend`/`hangSet`). That is the whole fitting: the holder hangs in the U by its own cap, the ends bear on the underside of the rim, and the shade hangs off them. Nothing is clamped, threaded, or hooked onto the ring, and the ends are not shaped at all — they are drawn long enough to come out the far side of the hoop, because an end that stops beneath it is an end the ring hides. **It got here by subtraction**, every step at the user's word: a bought cord stopper with three wires clamped under its nut between two packings → one wire with a closed eye in its middle → that wire bowed the wrong way up (a hammock sitting in the opening rather than an arch spanning it) → a hook bent into each end to catch the hoop → ends carried over the TOP of the ring instead of under it. Two things went with them and should not come back: a kit card (「コードストッパー」), and this option's `paperBody` — an edge to drape over is an edge either route has, so nothing here is route-specific, where the legs still are. **The U is open rather than an eye** for a reason worth keeping: the cord goes in sideways with the lamp already wired, where an eye has to be threaded from the free end of the cord. A slot's text is **route-neutral wherever it can be**: (3)'s three stop at the bench, and what the legs are then pushed into is named by the option's own `body`/`paperBody` (a slot has no `paperBody` of its own, and should not need one). **An option can carry a `note`** — a FOOTNOTE: last in the block, below the figure and the numbered work, marked with an asterisk, in the `wip` badge's voice. It is for a caveat about the WAY rather than a step inside it, and it belongs at the foot rather than under the body: up there it reads as another sentence of the body, which makes it a condition on doing this at all — and it is not. You do it, and then you may have to adjust it. ## Coordinate system and units

- **All dimensions in mm.** Ribs/koma/stand are a **Shape on the XY plane + extruded along Z** (= oriented for flat printing as-is).
- `outerR(p, t)`: height-normalized `t∈[0,1]` (0 = bottom, 1 = top) → outer radius in mm. The heart of the profile.

## Profile model (important)

The silhouette is a radius function connecting the **control-point array `p.pts = [{t, r, sharp?}]`** (ascending) via **monotone Hermite interpolation** (Fritsch–Carlson, `fukuroTangents`/`fukuroSpline`). To suppress warping and unwanted sharp curves, tangents are clamped to the same sign as the adjacent chords and to within 3×.

- **The outermost control points (`pts[0]` / last) = the opening = the neck radius.** Matching them here removes any wasteful flare (S-curve) from the neck into the lamp body.
- **The neck (kubi)** = a **vertical rectangle** outside the outermost control point (always a rectangle — never angled). Presence is independent top/bottom via `neckBot` / `neckTop`. The neck's height and overhang are adjusted with the ◇ handle in the section view (horizontal = overhang / vertical = neck height).
- **The lamp body (hibukuro)** = the curve **between** the outermost control points + bamboo-rib grooves. `fukuroRange(p)` gives its t range.
- **The design reference for the neck and tab is "the control-point radius"** (`openMin`/`bodyMinR`), and it **does not depend on whether the neck exists**. Toggling the neck does not change the tab (koma) size. On the side without a neck, the opening becomes the tab size (`komaR`).

## How big may a design get (`LIMITS`)

`src/config.ts` holds one table — `LIMITS = { height: [60, 2000], r: [10, 600] }`, in mm — and **everything that clamps a silhouette value reads it**: the section editor's ◇ drag and its `+` ghost, the typed radius field, the height scrub row, and `persist.ts`'s sanitize. Four separate copies is how you get a design that snaps back the moment you touch it somewhere else; the old code had exactly that (the editor clamped radius to 130, persist to 140, and persist's floor was 8 where the editor's was 10).

- **The floors are geometry. The ceilings are not.** `r = 10` is the wall: below it the opening is narrower than the rib's own core (`innerRi`), so the tab is wider than the mouth it has to come out of. Every height from 30 to 2000mm fails at r=8 and passes at r=10 — it is not a preference, and `check:manifold`'s cylinder family exists to keep it honest. The ceilings only stop a corrupt file or a fat-fingered typed value from asking for a metre-per-mm lantern; `check:manifold` sweeps the whole box watertight, and a festival 大提灯 is about ⌀1m, so ⌀1.2m is headroom, not a design decision.
- **These used to be 140–400mm and 10–130mm, and the wall was reachable by hand** — dragging a ◇ simply stopped. Widening them is what surfaced the slope-dependent lightening-window bug (see "STL watertightness" below), which was already reachable inside the *old* caps (a barrel scaled to r=130 at h=140 reaches dR/dy = 4.4) and invisible because nothing in any sweep scaled the control points.
- **The section view fits both axes**: `s = min(2.0, 520/H, (CX-30)/maxRadius(p))`. The first two terms are unchanged, so nothing that fitted before is redrawn at a different size; the width term only ever makes it smaller. Without it a wide, low body runs off the sides of the frame, taking the ◇ you are dragging with it. The 3D views needed nothing — `frame()` already sizes itself from `maxRadius(p)` and `p.height`, far plane included.
- **The height scrub row is non-linear** (`curve: 2.5` — see ScrubRow). Over 60–2000mm a linear slider spends 93% of itself above the sizes anyone builds; the curve gives 60–400mm half the bar, and one arrow-key step is still ~1mm at 205mm. Don't widen a range past a few multiples without it.
- **A bed-overflow hint of "→ reduce the body height" is only true when some height fits.** `heightLimit` returns 0 when none does — a ⌀1.1m rib overflows a 256mm bed at *any* height, and the parts are too wide, not too tall.

## Design rules (repeatedly raised points — fixed items so they don't need re-explaining)

Always obey the following when changing the shape. Removing these has caused reverts in the past:

- **The neck is always a vertical rectangle.** No slanted taper or angle. Its only role is positioning the opening.
- **Do not change the tab (koma) size based on whether a neck exists.** The neck widens the opening outward. Without a neck, opening = tab size.
- **The tab is a straight tongue.** No **outward** steps, hooks, or protrusions (the outer edge matches the koma outer diameter `kR` exactly and does not stick out).
- **Exception: the koma stop = the tab-tip dent** (added by reference to a real paper-lamp mold). **Both tabs** get an **L-notch cut out of the tip's inner (small-radius) corner** — `TAB_DENT_W`=6mm radially × `TAB_DENT_H`=6mm along the tab. The tip's inner edge pulls in to `innerRi + TAB_DENT_W` while the tab **base** stays at `innerRi`, and the koma's notch bottom is set to that same dented radius (`tabTipRi` → `notchR`), so **the wider tab base catches the koma's solid hub** and the koma cannot slide inward toward the lamp body.
  - **The koma is pulled out "outward" (toward the tab tip) after work.** The catch is a single inward step, not a clamp from both sides → nothing to ride over, so insertion/removal stays free. Blocking this makes the mold impossible to disassemble.
  - **The catch cannot drift between the two parts**: `ribOutline2D()` (which cuts the dent) and `komaShape()` (which cuts the notch) both derive from `tabTipRi()`. Change one and you change both.
  - `tabDented(p)` decides whether there is room (needs `tabLen > TAB_DENT_H+1` and `komaR - innerRi > TAB_DENT_W+2`); with no room it falls back to a plain straight tongue + full-depth notch. **`p.noTabDent` forces that fallback** — the cardboard papercraft sets it (see below).
  - Nothing here moves the stand: the dent only reshapes the tab tip, and `komaR()`/`standSlotSep()` do not depend on it.
  - **History**: this replaced an earlier "inward shelf on the top tab only" (`komaStop2D()`), which was removed. Don't reintroduce a shelf.
- **Tab width = board thickness `boardT`** (nominally equal to notch width). Notch width = `boardT + fit` (`fit` = print tolerance, added in `komaShape()`). Rather than "cramming it tight", match nominally and leave only the real-world clearance `fit`. With `fit=0` there is no gap, as before.
- **Hollow the rib's inner edge "only at the center"** (`ribInnerX()`). Real paper-lamp molds have this shape, to make the rib easy to pull out through the opening. **Do not bend the whole rib** (bending it end-to-end makes an impossible shape / a constant-width band gives an opening ≫ koma shape that never reaches the tab, splitting the rib in two). The top and bottom ends stay at **core `Ri`**, so the connection to the tab is unchanged. The hollow amount is the center depth × `RIB_CURVE_D` (real ones are about 20%). **It does not propagate to the outer edge, grooves, tab, koma, or stand** (only the inner material is reduced).
- **The groove's barb (tooth tip) leans toward the center = the equator** (not toward the opening). Do not place grooves right next to the opening; leave a half-pitch buffer (barbs at the very end don't work).
- **Grooves go evenly spaced across the entire lamp-body curve.** The curve always needs grooves.
- **Curves stay smooth.** Do not create wasteful sharp curves or S-warps derived from the control points (matching the outermost control point = the opening removes the flare at the neck).
- **Don't casually change the height or the stand.** Users reuse the stand. Dimension changes propagate to the joints (below).
- **The ribs have to come back out.** A rib leaves through one of the two openings, so the mouth's plane cuts **across** the plate — and because the plate's outer edge is single-valued in y, that cut is exactly one horizontal slice of the plate in its own frame, whatever angle it is held at. So what has to pass is the **band** (`outerR(y) − ribInnerX(y)`, worst over the body), not the plate's distance from the axis, and it has to fit the rectangle-in-a-circle bound `√(band² + boardT²) ≤ 2R` at the **wider** mouth. That is `ribPullFit(p)` in `geometry/rib.ts`; `PULL_CLEAR` (2mm) is practical slack on top of it, not part of the maths. The tabs never bind (they span `Ri..komaR`, and `komaR ≤ openMin`).
  - **It warns; it does not clamp.** Every way out — a wider opening, less bulge, more scoop — is a silhouette decision, and the app has no business picking one. It is the only viewport alert about a design that cannot be **built** rather than one that cannot be printed or cut, which is also why it is the last thing anyone would find out on their own: everything prints, fits the bed and is watertight, and you learn about it with a dry lantern in your hands.
  - **It belongs to both routes**, unlike the other two alerts — a cardboard mold comes out of the same hole — which is why the bottom-right corner became a stacked column instead of two mutually-exclusive cards (`Alert` in `TomoshibiStudio`).
  - **Deepening the scoop is the tempting fix and is not this one's to make.** `RIB_CURVE_D` is a real-mold proportion (see "Design rules" above and `ribInnerX`), and raising it to rescue a silhouette would move every STL the app has ever exported.

## Leg sockets on the bottom opening ring

The bottom ring doubles as the base of a stand for the **finished lantern**. Fixed items:

- **This was removed once (`f8447e2`, 2026-08-19) and put back deliberately.** That commit dropped the pads on the grounds that "the stand they were for is still undesigned", and left the ring a plain hoop with a marker tab. Do not remove them again on that reasoning: an undesigned stand is a reason to leave the sockets alone, not to delete the only feature that lets one exist.
- **The dimensions are CONSTANTS and stay constants (`LEG_N` / `LEG_D` / `TRI_R`).** The design says only **whether it wants sockets** — `p.legSockets`, one checkbox, and it ships **off**: standing the finished lantern on legs is one of the guide's three ways to light it, and the other two want a plain hoop. A missing flag reads as off too — in `sanitizeP` (via `DEFAULTS`) and in `ringLegs` alike, which is what keeps a restored design and its ring saying the same thing. They were never settings, and a leg count and rod diameter are not two more things to tune: a lantern either stands on legs or it doesn't, and a rod gets trimmed to the hole rather than the hole opened to the rod. Numeric controls for these were tried once and taken straight back out; don't re-add them.
- **`ringLegs(p)` is the single answer to "are there sockets".** `ringGeometry` and the inspector both call it, so the panel cannot claim sockets the part does not have. It returns `null` when the flag is off, and on the two ways the pads run out of room — the inward vertex crossing the ring's axis (`PAD_CORE`), and neighbours touching (`PAD_GAP`) — and the ring then falls back to a plain hoop. The small-opening case is reachable inside `LIMITS`: at the ⌀20 floor the pads fold through each other.
- **`ringLegsFit(p)` asks the second question without the first.** "You turned them off" and "they will not fit here" are different sentences and only one of them is news, so the hint appears only when the design asked for sockets and the opening refused.
- **The marker tab is cut exactly when the sockets are not.** Its whole reason is that the two rings would otherwise be the same hoop in two sizes (the barrel preset's rings are ⌀116 vs ⌀108). With sockets there is nothing to confuse, and the tab reaches 1.35mm past the nominal opening — a cost with no benefit. One `if`, not two independent decisions.
- **Verification is `check:manifold`'s leg-socket section, and the bore test in it is not optional.** The pads are separate closed shells merged into the hoop, so **edge counting is blind to a bore that got filled in** — the shell stays closed and a ring you cannot put a leg in passes. So it shoots a ray up each pad's middle and counts the faces over it (0 = open, 2 = capped), plus the two geometric conditions `ringLegs` exists to enforce and the fact that the flag being off really does cut nothing.

## Part joints (invariants for printing and assembling)

The mold is **rib × N + koma × 2 + stand**. Whether a reprint "fits the previous koma/stand" is determined by these values:

- **Rib tab ↔ koma**: board thickness `boardT` (the tab's thickness; notch width is `boardT + fit`) / `innerRi` (tab tip = notch-bottom radius) / `boards` (tooth count). If these three are the same, the tab plugs in to the same depth. `fit` only adds tangential fit clearance and does not affect engagement depth. `komaR` (koma outer diameter) only sets the koma's outward overhang and does not affect engagement.
  - **Implementation**: this engagement is **consolidated into the single function `innerRi()`** (`src/geometry/profile.ts`). Because `ribOutline2D()` (which makes the tab) and `komaShape()` (which cuts the notch) **call the same `innerRi()`**, the tab-tip depth and the notch bottom necessarily match. The only place that can break this invariant is `innerRi()` alone. Check the impact on both before touching it.
  - **Tab depth (engagement) and stand independence**: `innerRi()` is the legacy reference `nominalRi()` deepened toward the center by `TAB_DEEPEN` (tab tip / notch bottom). Meanwhile `komaR()` is based on `nominalRi()`, so **deepening the tab does not move komaR = the stand dimensions**. To prevent the wall between adjacent tab notches from getting thin and non-manifold from over-deepening, `ribCoreFloor()` (computed from the minimum inter-tooth wall thickness MIN_WALL) imposes a center-side limit.
  - **Notch bottom `notchR()` is also shared**: `komaShape()` (which cuts the notch) and `ribOutline2D()` (which cuts the tab-tip dent) both trace back to `tabTipRi()` → `notchR()`. With a dent the notch is **shallower** (its bottom sits at the dent radius) and the tab base, further in at `innerRi`, is what catches the hub. The deeper `TAB_DEEPEN`, the less room `tabDented()` has to find (both fight over the same inner space).
- **Koma ↔ stand**: `komaR` (saddle receiving radius = `komaR + SADDLE_FIT`) / `komaT` (koma thickness = post thickness) / `standSlotSep(p) = height + 2*tabLen - komaT` (post spacing) / `maxRadius(p)` (post height = floor clearance).
  - **Implementation**: `komaR()` (koma side) being called by `standGeometry()` (stand side) is **the single cross-edge bridging koma ↔ stand**. The post height traces back through `standSaddleH() → maxRadius() → outerR()`, so it **depends on the profile core `outerR()`** ⇒ changing the profile automatically moves the stand dimensions too (the substance of the caveat below).
- `komaR`/`tabDepth`/`innerRi` are based on the smaller opening (`openMin`). Note that **changing the opening radius changes the koma size**.

## STL watertightness (mandatory verification)

The ribs, koma, and stand must be **watertight (closed manifold)** or the print slicer breaks. After touching geometry, verify by sweeping across a representative parameter range. The test is the "shared count of undirected edges":

- 2 = closed (normal) / 1 = open edge / >2 = non-manifold. Additionally, no NaN vertices and no degenerate (zero-area) triangles.

**Verification is `npm run check:manifold`** (`scripts/manifold.mts`). It sweeps 3 presets × height/bamboo-rib diameter/pitch/board thickness/tolerance/plate count and inspects 41,472 parts, plus four narrower sections: Bézier handle editing, spiral winding, the **silhouette extremes** — the corners of `LIMITS`, which is the only section that scales the control points and therefore the only one that sees a steeply sloped face (see "How big may a design get") — and the **bottom ring's leg sockets**, the only section that runs `legSockets` off and the only one that tests a hole as a hole rather than as a closed shell (see "Leg sockets" above). Anything other than `0 FAIL` does not get merged. Additionally, for "refactors that shouldn't change the STL", use `npm run check:hash` (`scripts/hash.mts`) to diff vertex hashes before and after the change and prove the shape didn't move (usage is in the comment at the top of the script). **`check:hash` is only comparable within one three.ts version**: it hashes the position array in order, so a three upgrade that merely flips which diagonal splits a quad face rewrites every hash while the solid is untouched (this is exactly what r169 → r185 did to `boardGeometry`). Across a three upgrade, compare order-insensitive quantities instead — triangle count, signed volume, surface area, bounding box — which did stay identical for every part the sweep covered. The save feature's sanitize is verified by `npm run check:persist` (`scripts/persist.test.mts`), confirming that corrupt localStorage (broken pts, numeric strings, oversized boards, unknown version, etc.) recovers safely without crashing or producing a non-watertight koma. Papercraft **and the washi template** are `npm run check:paper` (`scripts/paper.test.mts`). The characters those PDFs print are `npm run check:glyphs` (`scripts/glyphs.test.mts`): the writer draws anything outside WinAnsi from the outlines in `src/pdf-glyphs.ts`, and a character with no outline is **dropped**, so a template that starts printing a new word prints a blank instead — on paper, where nothing else here is looking. It reruns `tools/pdffont`'s own collection over the same sources and fails both ways (a character with no outline; an outline nothing prints any more, which is the reworded-label drift `check:i18n` hunts in the dictionary). **`npm run typecheck`** (also the first half of `npm run build`) is the type gate — it covers `src`, `scripts` and the Vite config in one pass. **`npm run lint`** is the other non-geometric gate: ESLint with **only** the two React hook rules (`rules-of-hooks` / `exhaustive-deps`, both errors — see `eslint.config.ts` for why not `recommended`). A hook whose dependency array disagrees with its body is a stale closure — the viewport renders the design from three edits ago — and it is the one bug class here that is invisible in review *and* invisible to every geometric check. Opting out is fine, opting out silently is not: both existing exemptions carry an `eslint-disable` comment with its reason. The config's `files` is `src/**/*.{ts,tsx}` and its parser is typescript-eslint's, and neither is cosmetic: ESLint cannot READ a `.ts` file without the parser, and a `files` pattern that matches nothing reports "0 problems" exactly as loudly as a clean run — the one way this gate can fail is by going quiet. UI wording is `npm run check:i18n` (`scripts/i18n.test.mts`): because the dictionary is keyed by the Japanese string itself, **rewording a label does not make its translation stale — it deletes it**, and the app silently shows Japanese to an English visitor with nothing else in the build catching it. The script pins four shapes of that drift: a Japanese UI string with no EN entry (collected from every `t("…")` call, including calls nested inside template literals, plus every plain Japanese literal in `src/` — the latter also catches a label never wired through `t` at all), an **orphaned** EN entry whose key appears nowhere in `src/` (the fingerprint of exactly the reword above), a `{placeholder}` present on only one side (`t()` substitutes by name, so a dropped slot prints nothing and an invented one prints the braces), and a **duplicate key**, which JS resolves silently in favour of the last one — adding 「和紙」 for a guide card retranslated the inspector's own 「和紙」 heading, and 「竹ひご」 had been quietly overridden that way for some time.

Past failure causes and fixes: grooves too deep (→ cap depth at `higoD*1.5`), near-duplicate points on the barb's sharp flank (→ clean duplicate points before extruding), single-control-point presets (→ div-0 guard in `splineR` / denominators), a thin strip remaining in the lightening window at a waist (→ shrink/drop the window where it's thin), and **the lightening window cut straight through a groove on a steep face** (→ the band is slope-aware; see below).

**The lightening window's band is not a constant, and must not be turned back into one.** A groove is cut along the surface NORMAL (`grooveOuterPts`), so its tip does not land `depth` inside the smooth edge — it lands `depth × √(1+slope²)` inside, measured **in x at the tip's own height**, because the normal's y-component drags the tip up a face that is itself climbing in x. `lightenHoles2D` therefore takes `band(y) = max(bandW, grooveDepth(p) × √(1+slope²) + BAND_SOLID)`, worst slope over ±1.5mm so the 2mm chords between window samples cannot cut a corner either. `bandW` (11mm) stays the floor, so every design that was already clear of the notch keeps the exact window it had — 1144 of the 1152 parts `check:hash` covers are byte-identical; the 8 that moved are `barrel h140 higoD3`, whose surviving strip was **0.2mm**. `grooveDepth()` and `profileSlope()` live in `geometry/groove.ts` and are shared with the cutter for the usual reason: the window and the notch have to be talking about the same groove.

**Two kinds of earcut degeneracy (you will always hit these as you increase the point count; handled by `cleanPoly()` and `Y_STAGGER`)**:
- **Collinear points**: sampling the curve finely lays hundreds of "points on the same straight line" along a flat span. The side walls are built exactly along the point list, but **earcut drops collinear points**, so the boundary between cap and side wall shifts and becomes an open edge. → `cleanPoly()` thins out collinear points in addition to duplicates. **Apply it to both the outline and the lightening window** (the window side originally had no cleaning, and that was the hole).
  - **Implementation**: the point-list → `THREE.Shape` conversion is **consolidated into the single function `shapeFromPts(pts, holes)`** (`src/geometry/shape.ts`), which always applies `cleanPoly()` to both the outline and the holes. Build extrusion Shapes through here (writing it by hand forgets the cleaning and hits the degeneracy above — in fact `ribBandShape` had a copy of the cleaning that was missing only the collinear-point removal).
- **Same scanline**: if a lightening window's y-end lands at exactly the same height as an outline sample (`STEP=0.5mm`), the window corner and the outline vertex become collinear, producing a **zero-area triangle** and an open edge. → `Y_STAGGER=0.13` shifts the window's y-ends off the grid (`boardGeometry`'s `STAGGER=0.1` is the same fix).

## Papercraft (building from cardboard)

So it can be built without a 3D printer, the app outputs a **PDF** laying out each part's 2D outline at **A4 full scale (1:1)** (`src/papercraft.ts` → `paperPDF(p, matT)`), downloaded from the print view **as `tomoshibi_katagami.zip`, with the washi PDF beside it** — one download per route, the same shape the STL kit's ZIP has (see "Washi template"). **The paper side has its own fixed items too**:

- **The template PDF is the mold only — ribs and koma.** The washi panel is the other file in the ZIP, never pages spliced into this one; see "Washi template" for why, and for the `paperP` the cardboard copy must be cut from.
- **It is a PDF, and it used to be a self-contained HTML page.** That page carried a screen-only preamble — print at "Actual size / 100%", no margins, fit-to-page off, a "save as HTML" button — every line of which existed *because* the medium was HTML. A PDF is already A4 at exact size, so the preamble went with it and the app says the one remaining thing (the printer's 100% setting) beside the download — and that line is specifically the one **not** folded away by `KitNote` (see "What the export says about itself" below). Do not reintroduce an HTML route to get Japanese labels back; see the next point for the trade that was made knowingly.
- **The PDF follows the UI's language**, like the washi one — both are handed the app's own `t`. They were English-only until `pdf.ts` carried its own outlines, because `winAnsi()` **drops** what it cannot draw rather than mangling it: a Japanese translator yielded parts labelled `" ×8"` with the word silently gone and every structural check still passing. `check:paper` section 5 pins both halves — every part name must appear in the English PDF's drawn text, and the Japanese one must **draw** an outline for every character WinAnsi cannot encode (counted against the same pages rendered as SVG; dropping them is the old failure, and it satisfies every other assertion).

- **The shape comes only from `geometry.ts`'s pure functions.** Do not reimplement dimensions on the paper side (same rule as SectionEditor — if this drifts, the papercraft and STL produce different molds).
- **Don't cut the grooves (higo-me).** You can't carve a 0.5mm-precision V-notch into cardboard, so the outer edge is cut as a smooth curve (`ribOutline2D(p, k, { smooth: true })`) and the winding positions of the bamboo rib are shown with **dashed tick lines**. The positions come from the same `grooveList()` as the STL.
- **Don't open lightening windows.** Cardboard is light, and the windows only weaken it and add cutting work.
- **Material thickness `matT`** is the actual cardboard thickness the user measured. The koma's notch width is determined by it, so pass `{ ...p, boardT: matT, komaT: matT, fit: 0 }` **to all parts identically** (so the parts within the papercraft always mesh). Do not change the 3D side's `p`. `fit=0` (adding no print tolerance) is because cardboard's fibers crush as it goes in, so a nominal-exact fit meshes more firmly. For thick material it exceeds `maxBoards`, so **trim the rib count** and print a warning on the page.
- **Cardboard skips the tab-tip dent** (`noTabDent: true` in `paperParts`). The dent takes 6×6mm out of the tip's inner corner, which on cardboard is exactly where the tab is weakest — it tears along the flutes when pushed in. Cardboard trades the koma stop for tab strength and takes a **full-depth notch** instead; the koma is located by friction, which is enough because the fibers crush to a snug fit (`fit: 0`). `check:paper` asserts `tabDented(pk)` is **false** for every papercraft combination, so this cannot silently flip back.
- **The "koma wall is too thin" warning belongs to the APP, not the printed page.** The wall left between the koma's notches (`paperFit(p, matT)`) thins as the material thickens, and under half the material thickness it tears when hand-cut. Every way out of it — fewer ribs, thinner material, a wider opening — is a control in the app, so a warning on the printed page arrives after the sheet in your hand is already the wrong one. It is a **viewport alert** (bottom-right, in the column it shares with the bed-overflow one — the 3D-print route's counterpart — and with the pull-out warning; see "The ribs have to come back out" below for why that corner became a stack). The rib-count clamp stays on the page as well, because that one also states what the template you are holding actually contains.
- **Don't output a stand.** The papercraft outputs **only the mold itself (ribs + koma)**; the user provides their own stand. (A "cross stand" made of two cardboard strips crossed in an X was previously generated, but it was confusing and was removed at the user's request. The 3D-print stand — `boardGeometry`/`standGeometry` — is unaffected; it's a separate part for the printed mold.)
- **The full-scale check mark is an L — a try square, not a bar — and it goes wherever the layout already leaves room.** Shrinking under the printer's "fit to page" is the biggest source of accidents, and this is the only way to notice it with a ruler after printing; one mark answers for the whole job, because a printer scales every page of it alike. It is drawn **thick** (the `scale` style, 0.6mm) so a ruler's edge has something to line up against.
  - **It has two arms because a printer can scale the two axes by different amounts, and a horizontal bar cannot see that at all.** This is why every printable sewing pattern prints a *square* rather than a line ([Seamwork](https://help.seamwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/360004182294-The-test-square-on-my-digital-pattern-is-too-small), [Sussex Seamstress](https://www.sussexseamstress.com/sewing-tips-blog/how-to-print-and-assemble-pdf-sewing-patterns)). Do not reduce it back to one line.
  - **It is an L rather than a full square because the two axes do not cost the same.** Width is free — 200mm of it mostly goes unused — while height comes straight out of the parts. So the long arm (3in) goes across and the short arm (3cm) goes down: enough to catch an axis that scaled differently, no more. Over the `check:paper` sweep a full 3in square costs **800 sheets against 712**.
  - **Both units ride the SAME arms**, a tick where the metric figure falls and another where the imperial one does (`5cm`/`3in` across, `1in`/`3cm` down) — not a mark each. Patterns normally print one square labelled "10cm (4in)", but **4in is 101.6mm**, so that label is wrong by 1.6mm and the reader cannot tell which unit the square is true to. Two ticks on one arm are exact in both. **Don't pair 5cm with 2in**: 2in is 50.8mm, near enough that someone checking the wrong mark with an inch rule reads "just under 2in" and believes a bad print is fine.
  - **`scaleSpot()` places it, not `pageOps`** — it is a mark, not a part, so it belongs in room the layout has *already* left, and most designs finish with half a sheet blank. Candidates are the band's top-left plus the bottom-right corners the parts leave (the standard corner heuristic); parts are tested by **bounding box**, deliberately conservative, because printing the one mark the sheet's scale is judged by across a cut line is worse than spending a page. Over the sweep it lands on sheet 1 for 232 designs and sheet 2 for 128, and the template prints **712 sheets**.
  - **`TOPBAR` (36mm) is the fallback, not the plan.** When nothing anywhere fits, sheet 1 gives up that strip and the square goes to its top-left. Keep both halves: reserving the strip unconditionally cost **736** sheets, and the version before that *only* looked for gaps and silently shipped **16% of designs with no check square at all**. Neither failure is visible in the app — you find out with scissors in your hand.
  - **Ticks run inward off their arm.** Outward ones can reach past `MARGIN`, outside the printable limit `MARGIN` is set to, and a tick the printer clips no longer says where the length ends.
  - **`TOPBAR` shifts the CONTENT down, never the frame.** The frame is the sheet's trim box and has to land in the same place on every sheet to be a reference at all; a top line 36mm lower on sheet 1 than everywhere else reads as a misprint. `ft = joinsPrev ? y0 : MARGIN` is what keeps those two apart.
  - This strip replaced a `FOOTER` band that took 14mm off *every* page. `FOOTER` is 0 now: the parts get the full height between the margins. Measured over the `check:paper` sweep, the template went from **761 sheets to 726** (27 designs a page better, none worse) once `MARGIN` came in to 5 as well, and to **712** once the check square stopped reserving a strip of its own.
  - **Nothing else is printed per sheet — no title, no page number.** The seam codes already say which sheet meets which, and a footer band costs content height on every page to repeat what the codes state where it matters. **A part is split across sheets only when it doesn't fit on one page.** Page layout is two stages: (1) pack parts into rows → (2) pack rows into pages, and **a row that fits on one page is never straddled** (if it doesn't fit, start the next page at the beginning of that row = nothing to join). Each page is clipped by `top`〜`bot`, where `bot` is "if the next page is a continuation of the same row, `top+CH`, otherwise the next page's start position". Without this distinction, the head of the next row prints onto the previous page's bottom edge and a seam appears where nothing is joined.

- **Sheets are joined by a blue frame plus coded half-diamonds — the convention home-print sewing patterns use** (`STYLE.frame` / `STYLE.join` / `STYLE.jlabel`, all `#1769c8`; blue on a pattern sheet reads as "align", never as "cut"). Three rules, and each one is load-bearing:
  - **The trim box is drawn identically on EVERY sheet**, seam or not — it is a fact about the paper, not about the parts on it. Each edge runs the whole width or height rather than closing into a box: lay two sheets up and the upper one covers the lower one's corners, which is exactly where a box keeps all of its information, and only a long line makes a small angular error visible.
  - **Sheets BUTT at the trim box; there is no glue tab, and there must never be a second line.** A glue tab necessarily puts the join line a centimetre inside the trim edge, so every sheet at a seam carries two blue lines a centimetre apart — one to cut on, one to align on — and no drawing makes that pair unambiguous. One line does both jobs: cut both sheets on it, butt the cut edges, tape from behind. This is the join the reference patterns describe ("trim any white printer border first, then the frames coincide"), and it hands each continuation sheet 10mm of content back. **A seam is therefore read from the row** (`next.row === row`), not from an overlap — there is no overlap left to detect. `check:paper` pins every horizontal frame line to `MARGIN` or the trim edge; a third value means a line is marking something that isn't there, which is how a bottom frame drawn at the end of the *content* band was caught.
  - **The half-diamonds are OPEN chevrons**, ends resting on the frame edge, apex pointing inward. Open because the frame line already draws their base; closing them lays a second stroke along the very line the sheets are aligned by and thickens it. Two sheets laid up correctly bring opposed chevrons base-to-base and the ◇ closes — a millimetre out and it visibly doesn't, which is why this beats "line the two lines up" (two lines on top of each other hide half a millimetre of error).
  - **Two per seam, a fifth in from each end, each with a code** (`1A`/`1B`, `2A`/`2B`, …). One mark leaves the sheet free to pivot on it, so two pin rotation as well as offset, and the angle they fix is only as good as their spacing. The codes remove all doubt about which edge meets which without printing a sentence across the drawing. The left/right chevrons carry **no** code on purpose: the layout is one column wide, so those edges have no neighbour to mate with and are trim marks only.
  - **There are no corner registration crosses, and the `reg` and `glue` styles are gone with them.** The box's own full-bleed intersections are the crosses, and better ones (they run to the paper's edge). Do not reintroduce both — that was the cluttered doubled-up corner this replaced. A style in the `STYLE` table that nothing draws is a lie about what is on the sheet, so delete the entry when you delete the last consumer.

Verification is `npm run check:paper` (`scripts/paper.test.mts`). The test is not watertightness but **① being full scale (page dimensions = geometry.ts invariants) ② no parts missing from the layout ③ no NaN in the SVG** (a renderer silently ignores NaN, so you'd only notice after printing). It reads the pages through `paperPagesSVG()` — the same `pageOps` the PDF is built from, rendered as markup, which is far easier to assert on than PDF bytes; the PDF's own structure is section 5. It additionally checks that **notch width = material thickness** and that **the tab-tip dent is never cut on cardboard** (both directly cause "won't fit when assembled" and can't be caught by looking at the paper), and that `paperFit()` — the cheap summary the app's alert quotes — agrees with the formula and with what `paperParts()` hands the template. Section 4 of the same script covers the washi template (see below): arc length / panel width against an independent integration of `outerR`, tick count = groove count, guides drawn as guides, and the panel appearing on its own sheets and **not** among the cardboard pages. Section 5 covers the washi **PDF**: every xref offset must land on its object header (a hand-rolled PDF that viewers refuse or open blank), page count = the SVG's, MediaBox = A4, the page CTM is mm→pt, the 50mm ruler really is 50mm, and no non-WinAnsi byte reaches a `Tj` string — Japanese leaves through the outlines instead, and the same section counts those: as many glyphs drawn in the Japanese PDF as there are non-WinAnsi characters on the pages rendered as SVG.

## Washi template (cutting the paper skin before pasting)

Trimming the washi **after** it is pasted is the fiddliest step of the build and a torn wet edge shows, so the app also prints the paper skin's own flat pattern: **one panel = the surface between two adjacent ribs**, at A4 full scale. `washiGore(p, {side, end, span})` in `geometry.ts` → `washiParts` / `washiPDF` in `papercraft.ts`. Fixed items:

- **The sheet's length is the meridian ARC LENGTH `∫√(1+R'²)dy`, not the body height.** (A 205mm body needs 217mm of paper.) Cutting to the straight height is exactly the failure this template exists to prevent — `check:paper` asserts the arc length against an independent integration of `outerR`, and the page states the number in text.
- **Half width = `π·R(y)·span/N`** = half the rib-to-rib arc. The run is **`fukuroRange` only** (the neck carries no washi), so the ends land on the opening radius = the sheet just covers the opening.
- **Flattening is inexact by nature** (the lamp body has Gaussian curvature ≠ 0). The straight-axis gore makes the side edge slightly longer than the true meridian — worst ~4% at the steepest slope with 8 ribs, ~2% with 12, shrinking as `(π/N)²`. **Do not "fix" this by scaling the width**: the width is what makes the panel meet its neighbours. The page reports the max slack instead (damp washi takes it up; more ribs reduce it). The exact alternative (a cone-chain unroll with a curved axis) buys accuracy with extra horizontal seams and is not wanted for a lantern.
- **Allowances are the only additions**: `side` (overlap, added on **each** side — panels lap over each other on the rib) and `end` (cover, past **each** opening — folded over the opening ring). Defaults 3mm/3mm, adjustable in the UI, persisted like `matT`. The end overhang keeps the opening's width, which is exact because the neck is a vertical rectangle.
- **Guides are never cut lines.** The dashed guides mark the true rib lines (inset by `side`) and the opening lines; the short ticks mark the bamboo positions from the same `grooveList()` as the mold. They are traced from the same station list as the cut outline, so they can't drift off a sharp shoulder.
- **The template is traced, not glued on** — washi is translucent, so it is slipped under the paper. Never instruct the user to glue it to the material (that is the cardboard template's flow, not this one).
- **There is no on-screen preview of the washi panel, and it is not an oversight.** It is one sheet of one shape; it says nothing about the mold on screen, and the PDF is opened in a viewer that shows it better than a 190px dock did. A dock existed briefly on both routes (`WashiPreview` + `.pages-dock`) and was removed at the user's request — do not rebuild it, and do not draw the panel a second way for a thumbnail either. What the app says about the washi on screen is the 「和紙」 section's numbers.
  - **`washiPagesSVG()` survives that removal on purpose**: it is the SVG encoding of the very pages `washiPDF` writes, and **`check:paper` section 6 compares the two coordinate by coordinate** — every path and every label of both deliverables (3,942 paths over 36 designs), plus the fact that the UI language moves no coordinate. Section 5 pins the page counts. That comparison is what stands between a hand-rolled PDF and a file that disagrees with everything else here, and markup is the encoding you can assert on. The three encoding differences section 6 tolerates are named in its comment (2dp vs 3dp rounding, `text-anchor: middle` vs a pre-shifted x, `&lt;`); a fourth appearing means a renderer has drifted, not that the tolerance needs widening.
- **The washi template is not an output method, and has no download of its own.** It is not another way to make the mold — it is the paper skin you need on top of whichever mold you built, so it lives with the design settings (the 「和紙」 section: overlap / cover / panel size) and **rides along with whichever output you pick**, as `tomoshibi_washi_a4_beta.pdf` inside that route's ZIP: beside the STLs on the 3D route, beside `tomoshibi_katagami_a4.pdf` on the cardboard one. Do not re-add it to the output toggle: the toggle is mutually exclusive, and the washi is needed in both cases. One download per route, one deliverable per document — that is the shape, and a second CTA for the washi breaks the first half of it.
  - **It is its own PDF on the cardboard route too, not pages appended to the mold's template** (`paperParts` used to append them; it does not any more). The two are printed at different moments — the mold once, the skin once per lantern, often on different paper — and `pagesPDF` numbers and seams the sheets of *one* document, so splicing two deliverables together makes the seam codes span things that never join.
  - **Its panel width follows the rib count, so the cardboard copy is cut from `paperP(p, matT)`** — the *clamped* `pk.boards` = the mold that template actually makes. That is why `paperP` is exported: the studio hands the same `washiSrc` to `washiPDF` and to the dock, so the sheets on screen and the file in the ZIP are cut for the same mold. `check:paper` section 1 pins it (a clamped count must widen the panel), section 4 pins the split (the panel is on its own sheets and **not** on the cardboard pages), and section 5 pins it in the shipped bytes.
- **The washi template is marked `beta` — in the panel AND in the filename** (`WASHI_PDF` in `TomoshibiStudio`, one constant for both ZIPs and both notes). Flattening a doubly-curved surface is approximate by nature (see above) and how much a damp sheet takes up has not been checked against enough real builds. The dimensions are verified; the fit on a real lantern is not, and the panel readout is not a promise. The badge in the 「和紙」 section's hint is the same one, for the same reason, as the cardboard route's — but the **filename** is the half that matters, because the file outlives the screen it came from: it gets mailed, reprinted months later, handed to someone who never saw the app.
- **The PDF is labelled in whatever language the app is showing.** It carries no CJK font — embedding one would dwarf the file — but it does carry outlines for the characters it prints (see `pdf.ts` / `tools/pdffont`), so Japanese draws and only an unlisted symbol still folds through `winAnsi()`. Nothing dimensional depends on the labels — but keep the ruler note short in **both** languages (a long line collides with the right-aligned footer, which no CSS will save you from in a PDF).
- **Pages are built once as drawing ops** (`pageOps`) and rendered as SVG *or* PDF. Add a new page element there, not in one renderer — and the line/text styles live in the `STYLE` table (the stylesheet is generated from it) so the renderers cannot drift. The **in-app preview is not a third renderer**: `paperPagesSVG()` hands it the same `pageSVG()` markup the printable HTML is built from. It only asks for the CSS scoped (`styleCSS(".pages ")`), because those class names — `.note` among them — are the inspector's too.
- **All panels are identical, spiral winding included** (every bay sees the same helix), so one sheet is laid out labelled `×N`. Spiral only shifts the right-edge ticks by `step/boards` — that is `grooveList(p, gR, 0)` vs `grooveList(p, gR, 1)`, not a separate calculation.

## Build route (3D print vs cardboard)

Which route someone is on — `route`: `"stl"` / `"paper"` — is **app-level state held in `TomoshibiStudio` and persisted** (`persist.ts`, alongside the bed dimensions; anything but `"paper"` sanitizes to `"stl"`). It is not a per-export toggle: it is a fact about the maker, and the rest of the app branches on it.

- **The print bed only exists on the STL route.** Cardboard prints on A4 and anything larger simply **continues onto the next page, butt-joined** — so there is no size limit to warn about. `bedRules = route === "stl"` gates the overflow warning (in *every* view, not just the print view — it used to nag a cardboard user in the section view), the "→ lower the body height to Nmm" hint, and the warn colouring on the rib-length readout. Do not reintroduce a bed check that ignores the route: shrinking a design for a limit that route doesn't have is the bug this prevents.
- **The print view is a different kind of view per route.** STL gets the 3D plates (`buildPrint`); cardboard gets **`src/PagePreview.tsx`** — the template's own A4 pages as SVG over the (idle) canvas, exactly the way the section editor overlays its SVG; `buildScene` returns early for it, the same as for `"2d"`. **Its output is a document, so its preview is a document** — there is nothing spatial to show, and a WebGL page needs a canvas texture to say what a stroke of ink says for free. **Neither view previews the washi template** — it downloads as a PDF and is read as one (see "Washi template").
- **The preview never lays parts out itself.** It renders `paperPagesSVG()` — the same ops, through the same renderer, as the printable HTML — so the page count, the parts on each page and the part spanning two pages are the template's own answers. An earlier version packed the parts onto a field of A4 sheets of its own devising; the moment that disagrees with the template, the user believes the wrong one. On screen it is **not** full scale (hence the note, and hence the printed ruler).
- **The guide branches on the route too, and reads it through `paperP`.** On cardboard the page describes the mold that route *makes*: the material thickness becomes the board thickness, thick material clamps the rib count, the ribs are drawn with a **smooth outer edge and no lightening windows** (the template cuts neither), and the stand and ring steps are filtered out rather than reworded. Building it from the design on screen instead is the same bug the washi panel had — a page that counts ribs the template does not cut.
- **On a phone the sheets are ONE COLUMN and they TOUCH** (`.pages`, the `max-width: 860px` block). Both halves are the document's own structure, not a small-screen accommodation: the template's layout is one column wide, and consecutive sheets are **butt-joined** — cut both on the blue trim box, put the cut edges together, tape from behind. So the preview is the strip you will tape, in the order you will tape it, and a gap between two sheets would draw a join the finished template does not have. It reads: the half-diamonds of a seam sit directly above their partners and a part's cut line runs on across. This replaced a 2-up grid of 150px tracks, inherited from when the pane was `40vh`; the pane is now the whole phone at rest, so that was trading a readable 355px page for two thumbnails nothing could be read on.
- **`grid-auto-rows: max-content` is load-bearing, and `auto` is a bug.** This pane is a flex item with a *definite* height, and an `auto` row in a grid whose own height is definite gets sized against that height rather than against its contents — so with the inspector sheet pulled up the four rows came out **8.5px** each while every page still rendered 243px, and each sheet was drawn straight through the three below it. It was never narrow-only: on a 1440×900 desktop the same design gave 170px rows for 742px pages. A row has to be as tall as the sheet standing in it, and the sheet's size is the one thing here that is not ours to choose (the SVG carries A4's ratio).
- **The choice is offered on the welcome card**, and switched in the print view by the segmented chip **on the viewport, next to the mode tabs** — not in the inspector. It changes what the whole view *is*, so it does not belong at the bottom of a long scroll; the panel below it only holds that route's settings (bed size / material thickness) and names the route in its section hint.

## Onboarding (first-time visitors)

The app is published on a static host, so most first-time visitors arrive knowing nothing — and in English (`loadLang()` defaults to `en`). Two pieces cover them, deliberately split by the question they answer:

- **"What is this?" → `src/Welcome.tsx`**, one card shown on the first visit. The object on screen is not the lantern, it is the *mold*; the output is an STL or a full-scale paper template; the washi template comes with either one. **One card, not a step-through tour**: the app is a single screen with no empty state, and a spotlight overlay would have to track a viewport that stretches (the section view is a `preserveAspectRatio` SVG). Esc / backdrop / button all close it, and it never blocks anything.
- **"3D printer or cardboard?" → the two buttons on that same card.** The route decides whether the bed constrains the design at all, and the bed's warning starts nagging long before anyone opens the print view — so it is asked once, up front, rather than left to a toggle buried at the bottom of the panel. **Each button is also the start action** (pick → the card closes). Leaving without choosing is a **× in the card's corner**, not a third button on a footer row — it replaced a 「とりあえず見る」 that cost a whole row and read like a third option beside the two routes, when the escape from a modal is chrome rather than an alternative to the question. Esc and the backdrop still close it too. The card is presentational still: it takes `route` + `onPick`, and owns neither.
- **"How do I work the ◇?" → the legend at the top-right of `SectionEditor`.** It sat bottom-left until a wide, low body was found filling the bottom of the frame with the very drawing the legend explains; top-right it is clear of the section (which grows from the axis, on the left), tucked under the dimension chip, and level with the route chip on the other side. **On a phone it is a pill at the bottom-left that you tap open** (see "Narrow screens" below): at 300px wide against a 375px screen the card *is* the drawing, and it landed on the route chips too. It **redraws the canvas marks themselves** (control point, selection ring, add-point ghost, height handle, tangent handle) at legend size in the same shapes and colors, rather than describing them in words. Its content follows `editMode`: in curve-adjust mode the `+` ghosts are hidden and the point itself doesn't move, which reads as a bug unless the legend says so.

Fixed points:

- **The dismissal flag is `tomoshibi.welcome` (`persist.ts`), separate from the design state.** Exporting/importing a design must not carry "has this person seen the intro", and clearing one must not touch the other.
- **Auto-open keys on that flag ALONE — do not add "…and there is no saved design".** The autosave flushes on `pagehide`, so a first-time visitor who merely reloads before touching anything already has saved state and would never see the card, which is exactly the person it is for. The cost is that an existing user meets it once.
- **The three steps share ONE white panel with arrows between them; the route buttons are accent-bordered, full-width and stacked. Do not let the two converge again.** They used to wear the same treatment — white `--card`, a 1px `--card-edge`, an 11px radius, one box each — so the three things you cannot press looked exactly like the two you must, and they came first on the page. It is worst once the steps stack on a phone, where each becomes a full-width row with a box around it.
  - **One panel, not three.** A single `--card` ground on the `--panel` card reads as one block of explanation; three of them read as three targets. The `→` between the steps are what make it a sequence, and on a phone the *same* glyph is turned a quarter turn by CSS (`rotate(90deg)`) rather than written twice, and stays centred on the panel: the rows are three blocks in sequence, so the arrow belongs to the gap between them, not to the icon column.
  - **The buttons answer with the opposite emphasis**: accent border, a lift, a `:active` press, and a `→` saying that pressing goes somewhere. Both weigh the same — two equal answers to one question, not a recommendation and an alternative. Stacked full-width rather than side by side, so each is a button rather than a tile and the two captions stop wrapping to different heights.
  - **The cost is height**, and it came back out again: the shared panel and the stacked buttons took a phone from 560 to 656px, and dropping the closing hint paragraph (which explained the tabs and how to reopen the card — the ☰ answers the second half by being visible) brought it to **606px**, against 720 before any of this. The desktop is **536px**. At 375×667 the card reaches both ends: top 30, bottom 637. At 375×667 the card still reaches both ends (`margin: auto`, see below).
- **The cardboard button keeps its `beta` badge here.** This card is where someone chooses that route, and offering it without the caveat would sell a route that has had far less real-world building behind it.
- **The `☰` menu's 「はじめかた」 row is the only way back.** Once dismissed the card never auto-opens again, so don't remove that row (note the inspector is hidden in lit mode — that's fine, lit is a viewing mode).
- **Its layout lives in `index.css`, not in `narrow` ternaries, and its breakpoint is 480px rather than `useNarrow(860)`.** The component takes no layout props and should keep it that way; and the card is `min(560px, 100%)`, so what decides the layout is whether the **card** has room for three columns — about 460px of viewport — not whether the app is in its stacked mode, which a tablet with plenty of room would trip.
  - **The three steps stack into rows on a phone.** Three columns of ~85px is where this card fell apart: at 375px every caption wrapped to three or four lines and that row alone ran to ~330px, half the card. As rows — icon, then title and caption beside it — the block is ~200px and reads properly, and the card goes from 720px to **608px (ja) / 628px (en)**, which fits a 667px phone outright. The → arrows go with the columns: in a vertical list the order already says "then", and a column of ↓ would spend the height the stacking just saved.
- **The card is centred by `margin: auto` on the dialog, NOT by the overlay's `align-items`, and that is load-bearing.** Centred by `align-items`, a card taller than the window overflows in **both** directions, and a scroll container cannot reach what is above its start edge. The card is ~720px: on a 375×812 phone it just fits, and on a 375×667 one (an iPhone SE, or any phone held sideways) the wordmark was cut off with no way to scroll up to it — the first thing a first-time visitor sees, and the screen that asks the route question. An auto margin resolves to 0 once the free space goes negative, so the card starts at the padding edge and all of it scrolls into reach, while still centring wherever it fits. `align-items: safe center` would do the same job; don't replace it with plain `center`.

## Narrow screens (phones)

`useNarrow(860)` has always stacked the viewport above the inspector, but until 2026-08-29 that was the *only* thing that changed, and the result was an app you could read and could not work. Three measurements on a 375×812 phone say why, and they are the acceptance criteria for anything changed here:

| | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| ◇ hit target, section editor | **11px** across | **30px** (20px for the `+` ghosts) |
| smallest control in the inspector | **22px** (the "?") | 36px; sliders and value fields 44 |
| section view | 325px tall, drawn at 0.436 | **717px** at rest, drawn at 0.778 |
| inspector scroll area (of ~1250px of controls) | **216px** | 212px at `half`, **491px** at `full` |
| chip bar | 46px (ja) / **85px** (en) | 51px, identical in both |
| …with a viewport alert showing | **146px**, or **88px** in the print view | unchanged: the alert strip costs 37px, the sheet's stops absorb it |
| mm the design moves per px of ◇ drag | ran away — 40px took ⌀192 to **⌀392** | linear: 40px → ⌀243, the handle stays under the finger |

**The wide layout is untouched — deliberately, and it is worth keeping that way.** Every change below is behind `narrow`, and a 1440×900 desktop still renders the same `0 0 860 780` viewBox at the same scale with the same 24–25px targets. The section editor is the one place where a shared code path would have been tempting (a content-fitted frame is better at *any* size); it is not taken, because the frame is where every label position, the legend's clearance and half of this file's stated geometry live.

- **The section editor takes a `compact` flag** (`SectionEditor.tsx`). Nothing under it touches geometry — this file draws, it does not generate.
  - **The viewBox is fitted to the content** instead of being the fixed 860×780 frame. The fixed frame is more than twice the width the drawing uses, which is what pinned a phone at 0.44×. The fit is computed from the same quantities the drawing already has (`maxR`, `komaR`, the tabs, the axis stub) plus `FIT_PAD`, so a mark that moves takes the frame with it.
  - **Hit radii are derived from the measured on-screen scale** (`HIT_PT` = 30px, `HIT_ADD` = 20px), not written as constants in SVG units — a constant target shrinks with the drawing, which is the entire bug. The wide path keeps the old numbers as floors, so nothing there moves.
  - **The MARKS are sized the same way** (`GLYPH_PT` = 16px, `GLYPH_H`, `GLYPH_ADD`, `GLYPH_TAN`), and leaving them out of that treatment was a real bug for as long as the hit circles had it: a glyph written in SVG units renders smaller the further the drawing is scaled down, so a ◇ was 11.6px on a 1440px desktop and **8.6px on a phone** — the touch surface grew while the thing you aim at shrank. It also broke the legend, which **redraws these very marks at legend size**: a canvas mark half the size of its own legend entry reads as a broken UI, not a small one. Stroke weight and corner radius ride along with the size, and the label offset is `rPt + 9.5` rather than a constant 15 (on the wide path that IS 15 — a bigger mark would otherwise be drawn straight through its own number).
  - **The pane is measured with a layout read AND a ResizeObserver, not the observer alone.** An observer only delivers for an element the browser is laying out, so a hidden or throttled tab leaves it silent — and the `pane.w === 0` fallback is scale 1, which hands a phone the small targets this path exists to remove. This was hit for real, not hypothesised.
  - **The legend steps out of a short pane** (`showLegend`, gated on the measured `pane.h`). At the sheet's tallest stop the drawing is a 140px sliver and a 34px pill is a fifth of it, parked on the very drawing it explains, for someone plainly working in the panel. Below 220px the drawing is context, not a work surface.
  - **A drag freezes the screen→model mapping at pointerdown (`freezeMap`), and this is a correctness fix, not a nicety.** Both halves of that mapping depend on the design being dragged: `s` (mm → SVG units) shrinks as `maxRadius` grows, and the content-fitted viewBox stretches as the silhouette widens. Read live on every move — which is what the code did — that closes a positive feedback loop, and dragging one control point 40px to the right took the design from **⌀192 to ⌀392**; the handle left the finger behind. Frozen, millimetres-per-pixel is whatever it was when you touched down and 40px moves it to ⌀243, exactly the 51mm the scale says. **The wide path barely showed this** (its viewBox is fixed and `s` is pinned at 2.0 until the body passes ~200mm radius), which is precisely why the bug arrived with the fitted frame and had to be fixed here rather than left for someone to find on a phone.
  - **Compact drops the NAMES, never the NUMBERS.** Out go the region labels (首/火袋/首 — the colour bands already say it, and they hang off the LEFT of the widest part of the body, which `cx0` reserves nothing for, so they would be clipped as well as costly), the 羽根板 caption, the 開口/首 tag and the 火袋の高さ caption. Every mm readout stays: they are the answer to "how big is this", which is the question the drawing exists to answer, and the inspector that would otherwise state them is behind the sheet. **They were briefly dropped too, and that was wrong** — the drawing became a shape with no dimensions on it anywhere. They also cost the frame almost nothing, because `FIT_PAD.r` reserves the width of a label on the right unconditionally; widening that reservation from 64 to 78 (the mark now pushes the label further out) took the drawing from 0.778 to 0.756, a 3% scale for every dimension on screen.
- **The mode/route chips are a bar in FLOW above the viewport (`chipBar`), not an overlay on it — and in it they are two `<select>`s, not eight chips.** Floating, the two rows were ~100px of a 357px pane — 28% of it, laid over the top of the silhouette, which is where the top opening's ◇ is; the drawing was larger that way and one of its handles could not be touched. In a bar they covered nothing, but eight chips still wrapped to two rows (85px) in English. **Do not shorten the labels to force one row** — these are the app's top-level navigation, and a phone is where you can least afford to guess what an abbreviation meant. As dropdowns the same two choices are one row in every language, and the row has space left for the "?" and the language toggle.
  - **They are NATIVE `<select>`s, deliberately.** On a phone that opens the OS picker — a better touch target than anything hand-rolled here, with keyboard and screen-reader behaviour already correct and no focus-trap or outside-click code to own. Only the closed state is styled (`.tab-sel`); the open list is the platform's, which is the point. The `beta` badge becomes text in the option, because an `<option>` cannot carry markup. Filled accent for the view (this is where you ARE, so it reads like the pressed tab it replaced), plain for the route (a setting, not a location).
  - **The header's `☰` lives at the bar's top RIGHT on a phone** — it was a "?" and a language toggle until both became rows in it — not in the panel (there is no panel header there) and not in the sheet's bar (it would be the only thing in the app that scrolls out of reach at rest). This strip is on screen in every view and at every stop of the sheet — **including lit**, where the whole inspector is hidden and the "?" used to be unreachable altogether.
  - **The language toggle used to be a button in this row and is now a row in the `⋯` menu** (see below). While it was here it had to take its height from `min-height` rather than from padding + line-height: its label is "EN" in one language and "日本語" in the other, and the taller CJK line box made the whole bar 16px taller in English than in Japanese for the same one row of controls. Any control whose label changes with the language has the same problem.
  - The consequence is that `.pages` (the cardboard preview) has **no chip clearance to leave on narrow**; its padding-top is 12, not the ~120 the wide path needs.
  - **Lit keeps the view control and drops only the route one.** The inspector is hidden in lit, so on a phone this bar is the only way back out of it.
- **The inspector is a bottom sheet, and the viewport is simply everything the sheet is not using.** It rests at `peek` — its grabber bar and the live summary, and nothing else, 44px — so the section editor gets **717px of an 812px phone** and is the main thing on screen, which is the point. Pulling the sheet up trades that back one stop at a time (`half` = 45% of the shared budget, `full` = the budget minus `MIN_VIEW`), and the viewport is a plain `flex: 1 1 auto` at every stop. There is no `40vh` any more and **lit needs no exception**: it renders no sheet, so it already gets everything.
  - **The stops are fractions of the budget the sheet SHARES WITH THE VIEWPORT, not of the window.** Those look equivalent and are not — the chip bar above them is one row in Japanese and two in English, so a window-relative `full` gave Japanese a 76px section view and English **37px**. Budget-relative, both get exactly `MIN_VIEW` (140px). The budget is measured as `main + aside`, whose *sum* is invariant while the sheet animates, which is also why observing both does not re-render every frame.
  - **`peek` is the bar alone, and it is measured rather than assumed** (`barRef`) — the summary it carries wraps on a narrow enough screen. It briefly included the pinned CTA as well, which made it 128px: at that size the sheet is 16% of the phone doing nothing but resting, and the section editor is what that space is for. The CTA is now one tap away, and outside the print view the one it shows was navigation the bar's own view control already offers. **The trade is the print view**, where the download button is the mode's whole point and now needs the sheet opened first.
  - **The CTA stays PINNED AT THE BOTTOM, and the DOM order is the plain one** — bar, scroll area, footer, on both layouts. `peek` needs no reordering: the sheet is exactly bar + CTA tall there, so the scroll area between them collapses to zero, and every stop above it grows that and only that. It was briefly built the other way, with the footer given `order: 1` so it sat directly under the bar. That put a full-width button between the drag handle and the first control and left the list sliding under it with nothing to disappear behind — **a half-cut row reads as a rendering fault, not as a scroll**. Both edges of the scroll area now draw a rule (the bar's `borderBottom`, the footer's `borderTop`, the latter dropped at `peek` where the two would meet as one doubled line).
  - **The scroll area carries NO vertical padding on a phone**, and that is not cosmetic: `min-height: 0` floors a border-box at its padding, so 4+14px of it was 18px this element could not shrink past — and `peek` then overflowed the sheet by exactly that and cut the bottom off the CTA. The spacing it bought is given back by the wordmark block at the end of the list. The aside is `overflow: hidden` on narrow for the same family of reasons: at `peek` the pinned CTA sits past the sheet's own bottom edge and is clipped rather than left hanging.
  - **What `peek` shows is the live summary.** It moved out of the pinned footer and into the sheet's bar because it is the readout you watch *while dragging a ◇* — it has to be on screen at rest. The grabber pill is positioned against the bar rather than laid out in it; both were briefly centred on the row minus the "?" and the language toggle, which put them at 37% of the sheet and read as a mistake rather than as a handle. The wordmark went the other way, to the **end** of the scroll area: putting it at the top would spend the first 40px of every pull, the most expensive space in the app, on identity, for someone who pulled the sheet up to reach a control.
  - **The grabber is a `div` with `role="button"`, not a `<button>`, and that is load-bearing.** `onSheetDown` bails out of anything inside a real `<button>` so any button in the bar stays pressable — so a `<button>` grabber is the one part of the bar you cannot pull, which is exactly what it was while the summary text lived inside one. The pointer path already treats a press that never travels (`< SHEET_TAP` = 6px) as a tap that cycles to the next stop, so the div only has to add Enter/Space.
  - **Only the bar drags; the scroll area scrolls.** Arbitrating "is this finger scrolling the list or pulling the sheet" is the genuinely hard part of a bottom sheet and it is the one thing a library (`vaul`) buys that this does not. Measured, that library costs **+21.4 kB gzip and 11 packages** against a 105 kB app and a four-dependency policy; this sheet cost **+0.94 kB** and no packages. If the scroll/drag hand-off is ever actually missed, `vaul` is the swap — the structure above it does not change.
- **The viewport alerts move in flow too, between the viewport and the panel — folded into a strip you tap open.** A three-line card is a quarter of the pane and it lands on the bottom opening's ◇ — an alert reading "widen the opening in the section view" while covering the handle that widens it. But in flow it comes out of the *same* budget as the inspector, and expanded it is ruinous there: one open alert cut the panel's scroll window from 261px to **146**, and in the print view — taller footer, and the koma-wall warning fires on the **default** cardboard design — to **88px**, i.e. 7% of 1333px of controls. That is the "every control exists and there is no room to reach one" failure this layout was written to remove, arriving through the thing meant to help. Collapsed the strip costs ~36px and still says it: the alert tint, the ⚠, the first alert's headline (the sentence naming what is wrong — the "→ do this" hint is what the tap is for) and a `+N` when more are waiting. **Do not make it open by default to be safe** — that is the 115px band again, and it takes the panel with it.
  - **The alerts are built as DATA (`alerts: {key, head, hint}[]`), not as markup.** The strip has to count them and quote one headline, and a fragment can answer neither question. This also retired the separate `hasAlert` predicate the fragment needed — a fragment is truthy even when every card inside it is false, so gating on it rendered an empty bordered band.
- **The selected ◇ gets a contextual bar in flow, between the viewport and the alerts** (`ui/PointBar.tsx`), and it is a fix for a trade rather than for a tap count. `PointCard` sits 185px into a 1333px list and needs no scrolling once the sheet is at `full` — but `full` is `MIN_VIEW`, so reaching it drops the section view from 717px to **140** and you cannot see the point you are editing; at `half` the window is 212px and the 256px card does not fit either. The bar costs **57px while a point is selected and nothing otherwise** (717 → 660, or 623 with an alert strip below it), and it dismisses itself because tapping empty canvas already clears the selection.
  - **The H field is 56px, sized to its content, and it has a `min-width`.** It was `flex: 1 1 auto`, which handed it every spare pixel — 99px of box for a three-digit number, on the one bar whose whole point is not to spend the screen; `margin-right: auto` takes the slack now, which also separates the point's VALUE from the buttons that act on it. The floor matters more than the width: a 2000mm design puts FOUR digits in there, the input is right-aligned, and an overflow shows the END of the number — `000` for 2000, silently, in a field you are about to type into. Under 360px the row cannot hold that and everything else, so the identity label is what gives (see below). Shrinking every target instead buys the same width and makes every one of them worse.
  - **It carries H and NOT R.** The section view already prints every point's radius beside its ◇, on both layouts and in compact, so a radius field here was the same number twice 20px apart for 63px of a 375px row; height position is the one dimension the drawing states nowhere. The radius is set by dragging, which is the app's primary gesture. The tag is a mono letter and the value a number, so this row cannot change height between Japanese and English. Everything in it is a 44px target — it exists so that nobody has to go and find the 44px controls in the sheet.
  - **All three glyph buttons carry a 9px caption under the mark** (◇ なめらか / ■ 角 / ◠ カーブ). The glyphs are the marks the section view already draws, so they carry the meaning — but a row of bare glyphs is exactly the discoverability problem the ☰ menu's rows avoid by being labelled, and captions cost 4px of width (42 → 46) and none of the height. The longest is 「なめらか」 at 36px; English tops out at "Smooth" at 34. The glyph `<span>` is `aria-hidden`, so the accessible name is the word alone rather than "◇ Smooth".
  - **The curve-adjust mode is a TOGGLE, not a segmented pair**, and that is the semantics rather than the width: "move" is the editor's resting state and "curve" is a thing you turn on, so a pressed button says it in one 44px square where two segments cost 88. It sits after the point's own properties behind a **rule**: the row is three groups — the point's own values, the editor's mode, the destructive action — and in the same `.seg` skin, ◠ hard against the ◇/■ pair reads as a third exclusive option in it however much air is between them. 6px of margin was tried first and did not say it. The rule before delete goes with delete under 360px (`.ptbar-sep--del`); a divider left behind separating nothing is worse than no divider. **The entry rule is shared**: `makeSetMode` in `pointEdit.ts` bakes the Bézier handles on the first entry into curve mode, which is shape-neutral but must happen exactly once, from whichever surface asked.
  - **`PointCard` renders NOTHING on a phone** (its `compact` prop is an early return). Everything it held is either in this row or is done by dragging the ◇ itself. The last thing left in it was the **radius**, and the radius is exactly what dragging a ◇ sideways sets, printed live beside the mark by the section view — a field for it bought precise typing and nothing else, in a section you had to pull the sheet up and scroll to reach. The inspector list goes from 1333px to **1057**. The wide layout keeps the whole card; it has no bar.
  - **Delete therefore lives in the bar unconditionally**, and the 360px rule drops the **identity label** instead: the section view already rings the selected ◇, so the label is the one thing in the row that is also said elsewhere, while delete now exists nowhere else on a phone. The label is a `<span>`, not a button — there is nothing left for it to open.
  - **The legend's `sel` row changes with it**: 「選ぶ → 右パネルで編集」 is a wide-layout fact, and on a phone it now reads 「選ぶ → 下のバーで編集」. A legend that names a panel the layout does not have is worse than no legend.
  - **Delete IS in it**, separated by a rule and in warn colour. One tap from a persistent bar is only acceptable because ⌘Z covers it; do not add a second destructive control on the same reasoning.
  - **The editing rules live in `ui/pointEdit.ts`, not in either surface.** Two things now edit the same ◇, and the mm→`t` conversion with its neighbour clamp (±0.04, which is what keeps `p.pts` ascending for `fukuroTangents`), the "more than two points" delete guard, and clearing the selection on delete are exactly the invariants that would rot if they were written twice.
- **The inspector's chrome is compact**: no panel header at all (its two buttons moved to the sheet's bar), tighter padding, and the three-row summary grid folded to one mono line. Header + footer were 237px of a 455px panel — more than half of it — which is the other half of "nothing can be operated". The one thing the compact summary keeps is the rib length's **warn colour**, which is the only signal in that block rather than a fact.
- **Every control in the inspector is a touch target, and almost all of it was free** (the narrow block at the END of `index.css` — see the comment there for why the end). They were all mouse-sized: the ± squares 26px, the value buttons 62×24, the sliders a 24px strip, and the "?" — the only way back to the onboarding card — 22px across. But `.scrub-row` was **already** `min-height: 44px` and `.row` already came out at 40: the controls simply were not filling rows the layout had reserved for them. So the sliders and value fields grow into their own row, and the ± squares keep their 26px box and get a 40×44 `::after` overlay (the same glyph-vs-hit-circle split the section editor draws) — nothing in that block costs a row.
  - **The header button was the one exception and is not one any more: `.icon-btn` is 36px in its BASE rule, on both layouts.** It was 22px wide / 36px narrow, which made "too small" a phone problem. It is not — 22px is small for a pointer too, this button is the app's whole menu and the only way back to the onboarding card, and the wide panel header loses nothing by it being bigger because the row's height comes from the 44px wordmark beside it. A control too small to hit with a finger is usually also too small to find with a cursor; prefer fixing the base rule to adding a narrow override.
- **What is NOT done, and why.** The `+` ghosts sit at the midpoint between two control points, so they are ~19px from each on a phone; they cannot be given the same target without swallowing the points, and they keep the smaller one. Adding a control point is the rarer action and the ◇ it sits between is the common one. Don't "fix" this by growing `HIT_ADD` — measure the point-to-ghost distance first.

## What the export says about itself (`KitNote`)

Under each export CTA there used to be a paragraph: duplicate the koma in your slicer, a config.json rides along, the washi PDF is bundled, print at 100%. Five lines on a phone — about 95px of a sheet whose whole job is to leave room for the drawing, and 4 lines of a 336px panel on the desktop.

**The fix was not shorter prose. It was noticing that none of it helps you decide to press the button.** "Duplicate the koma", "set the printer to 100%", "a config.json is included" all matter once you *have* the file, in another application. A paragraph of them pinned above the button is Apple HIG's overwhelm-upfront in miniature. So:

- **Nothing at all is drawn until the export has actually run.** Following the observation above to its end: *every* line here is about the file you already have, in your slicer or at your printer — so before the download there is no reader for any of it, and the whole block appears as the download's own confirmation (`success-feedback`), manifest already open. On a 375×812 phone that is the pinned footer at **67px instead of 131**, i.e. 509px of inspector scroll instead of 445, at every sheet stop; on the desktop the panel gets the same 64px back. **Do not put it back on screen "so people see it"** — a paragraph read on the way past the button is the thing this section exists to have deleted, and it was measured at five lines.
- **The split within it is by consequence, not by length.** Loud once it appears: the one step that ruins the output if it is missed — printing at anything but 100% on the cardboard route, printing one koma where two are needed on the STL one. Everything else is the ZIP's **manifest**, reference material, folded behind a `同梱物` disclosure.
- **`kitNote` is three-valued (`null` / `"open"` / `"shut"`), and that is not a style choice.** Two booleans (downloaded? open?) admit "folded but never downloaded", a state this has no drawing for. The CTAs set `"open"`; the disclosure toggles the other two.
- **A list, not a sentence.** The manifest is one line per file, filename in mono. Prose has to be read; a list is scanned, and "what is in this ZIP" is a lookup, not an argument.
- **Both layouts, not just the phone.** This is an information-architecture change rather than a narrow-screen accommodation, and keeping prose on the desktop would mean two copies of the same copy drifting apart. It is the one place in this branch where the wide layout deliberately moved.

## The header's `☰` (`ui/Menu.tsx`)

One menu at the top right — on the phone's chip bar, and in the wide panel header, the same button in both. It holds what acts on the **app** or on the design **as a file**: the intro card, the language, backup save/restore, and reset. It replaced a `?` and a language toggle standing in the row itself.

- **It is a `☰` even though nothing in it is navigation, and that is a knowing departure from the convention** (☰ = navigation drawer, ⋯/⋮ = overflow of actions). By the convention this should be `⋯`: the app's navigation is the two selects immediately to its left, they stay visible, and the view one is filled accent precisely because it says where you are. What decided it the other way is that `⋯` is materially harder to find — it reads as "more options for the thing next to me" — while `☰` is read as "this app's menu" by everyone, which is what the contents are. The usual case against hamburgers (NN/g) is about hiding NAVIGATION, and nothing navigational is hidden here. **Do not put a destination in this menu** — that line is what the trade rests on.
  - **An OVERLAY is not a destination, and that is the distinction the menu now runs on.** Both 「はじめかた」 and 「作り方」 open a document over whatever you were doing, and closing it puts you back exactly there — the view underneath never changes, and neither does the URL, the tab strip, or anything else that says where you are. A destination would be a place the app can leave you *in*, and there is still not one in here. That is the test to apply to the next row somebody wants to add: not "is it a page?" but "can you end up parked in it?" The button is a rounded SQUARE for the same reason it is at that end of the row: it stands among `.tab-sel` selects with 9px corners, and a lone circle read as a different kind of control.
- **What justified folding anything away was measured, not assumed.** On a 375px phone the chip bar in **English** came to exactly 375px — view select 99 ("Assembly") + route select 144 ("Cardboard (beta)") + the two buttons 88 + 24 of gaps + 20 of padding — with its flex spacer collapsed to **zero**. Japanese, whose labels are shorter (64 / 129), had 55px spare. One 36px button in place of those two returns 52px / 47px, and the row that carries the app's top-level navigation stops being full in the language that fills it. **The binding language here is English, not Japanese** — the opposite of the usual assumption in this file, and the reason to re-measure both before adding anything to that bar.
- **Undo and redo deliberately stayed out**, and `Toolbar.tsx` is now those two and nothing else. They are the recovery path for a direct-manipulation editor that fills the screen — the frequent case an overflow menu exists to make room *for*. They do not fit the bar either: in English the right-hand cluster has 88px to spend and `⋯` + undo + redo is 124.
- **Reset is separated and states its consequence.** It sits below a rule, in warn colour, with the sentence that used to be its `title=` as a second line — a phone has no tooltips, and a destructive row in a menu needs the separation (`destructive-nav-separation`).
- **The two file rows are named for what they are FOR** — 「バックアップを保存」/「バックアップから復元」, not 「設計を書き出す/読み込む」. There are exactly two moments either is reached: browser data gone, and restoring the `tomoshibi_config.json` out of a kit ZIP downloaded months ago — which is also the ONLY reader that file has, so neither row may be deleted while the ZIP still ships it. `KitNote`'s manifest already calls it 設計のバックアップ; the menu now says the same word.
- **The language row is a setting, not a verb**: the row is named 「言語」 and the right-hand side shows what it would become. The old control was a button captioned with its own opposite (「EN」 while in Japanese), which also made the bar 16px taller in one language than the other.
- **The file `<input>` lives in `TomoshibiStudio`, not in the menu.** A menu row unmounts the instant it is clicked, and an input that is gone cannot open its own dialog. For the same reason the row runs `onClick()` *before* closing.
- **Focus moves into the menu only when it was opened from the keyboard** (`detail === 0` on the click is how Enter/Space announces itself). Focusing the first row after a tap lights it up under a focus ring for someone who never asked; not focusing it at all leaves a keyboard user Tabbing into a list that just appeared. Escape is handled on the wrapper rather than on the rows, because after a pointer open the focus is still on the trigger.

## Conventions

- **Comments are in English** (match the existing style). Write units and intent for formulas and dimensions.
- **`<html lang>` follows the dictionary** (`useLang`). `index.html` can only ship one value and the app restores Japanese from localStorage, so without the effect the document claims to be English while showing Japanese. Not cosmetic on a phone: `lang` is what a mobile browser picks a CJK font fallback from and what a screen reader picks a voice from.
- **UI strings are Japanese, and the Japanese IS the dictionary key** (`i18n.ts`). So **editing any user-facing wording means editing `EN` in the same commit** — the old key does not warn, it just stops matching. Run `npm run check:i18n`; it fails on both halves of that mistake (the new wording with no translation, and the old entry left stranded).
- Keep `geometry.ts` as **pure functions** (don't bring in React/DOM). Otherwise the section view and STL will drift.
- So the shape matches across views, always draw the section view (SectionEditor) using `geometry.ts`'s functions (**including the dimensional constants** — don't reimplement them yourself). The groove half-width is consolidated into `grooveR(p)` (this used to be split between `higoD/2+0.15` and `+0.25`, and the section view drew a thinner groove than the STL).
