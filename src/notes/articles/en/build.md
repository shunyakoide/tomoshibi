# How a washi lamp comes together

## What you need {#what-you-need}

### Materials

- a mold (3D printed, or cut from cardboard)
- bamboo strip
- paste (wood glue works)
- wire
- a lamp socket
- washi
- something to fix the bamboo with (thread or tape)

### Tools

- two brushes: one to press the washi onto the bamboo, one to apply the paste
- a spray bottle
- pliers, for bending the wire
- a razor or scissors, for cutting the washi

Use whatever you already have around you. The tools for the paste are not essential: a cotton bud puts it on well enough, and washi can be pressed down by hand.

![Brushes and a spray bottle](notes/tools.jpg)

## Watch a video first {#watch-first}

Before starting, it helps to search YouTube for lantern-making videos and watch how it is done. The process is much easier to picture afterwards.

## Assemble the mold {#mold}

Put together the mold you printed, or the one you cut from cardboard.

![The printed mold parts](notes/printed-parts.jpg)

![The 3D-printed mold assembled, on its base](notes/printed-mold.jpg)

The cardboard route has no base, so you get the mold for the body only.

Another way might be to make a hole in the middle of the top and bottom koma, put a rod through it and work with the mold on its side.

![The mold cut from cardboard and assembled](notes/cardboard-mold.jpg)

![The edge of a cardboard rib. The bamboo sits in the notches](notes/cardboard-rib-notches.jpg)

For the grooves in the cardboard, make a short straight cut sideways with the tip of your scissors, then form the groove with a finger. That comes out well.

## Add the rings {#rings}

On the 3D-printed route you can print the rings too. On cardboard you cannot, so you bend them from wire.

![A printed ring, this one with three holes for the legs](notes/leg-socket-ring.jpg)

The holes take the legs that hold the light up. How the legs mount has a note of its own: [Legs and how to mount the socket](note-sockets).

![Wire](notes/wire-coil.jpg)

The wire in the photo is 2mm, but use whatever thickness you like. Some thickness to it gives the rings their strength and holds them steady.

The PDF carries a template for the rings, so bend the wire to it with pliers. Mind the wire's ends, which can be sharp.

![Bending the wire to the line on the template. I am bending it by hand here; around a pen it comes out neater](notes/wire-ring-bending.jpg)

![The finished rings](notes/wire-rings-done.jpg)

![The wire rings fitted onto the cardboard mold](notes/cardboard-rings.jpg)

## Choosing the bamboo {#bamboo}

Which bamboo you can get will differ by country and region, but I would recommend the thinnest and longest you can find, because it bends more easily.

![Bamboo strip](notes/bamboo.jpg)

Some is sold straight. In that case, coil it into a circle, clip it, and leave it in water for a good while: it takes on the curve and the work gets easier.

![Coiled, clipped and left in water](notes/bamboo-soaking.jpg)

## Winding the bamboo {#winding}

There are broadly two ways: making one closed ring at a time, or winding a single length in a spiral.

I found making rings one at a time easier. With a spiral, unless you fix it down as you go, the mold turns or the bamboo slackens. That is a matter of skill more than anything, so use whichever you prefer.

### One ring at a time {#winding-rings}

Wind the bamboo into the mold's groove. Mark it where it comes round to itself, then take it off the mold. Wrap thread round it in your hands to hold it, put it back on the mold to check the size is right, and cut off what is left over.

![The overlapped ends bound with thread](notes/bamboo-lashed.jpg)

![The bamboo wound onto the mold](notes/bamboo-wound.jpg)

![The bamboo wound onto the cardboard mold](notes/bamboo-wound-cardboard.jpg)

As for fixing it: looking into how lanterns are made, I kept seeing tape or thread, and I use thread as a rule, because it seems less visible once the lamp is lit. What I wind on is sewing machine thread. The Akari lights I was looking at were held with thread too. Look inside one if you get the chance to see a real one — it is beautifully done and you cannot pick it out at all.

### Winding a spiral {#winding-spiral}

If you can get hold of a very long strip, this way may be easier. If it is not long enough, joining pieces together first saves trouble later.

Fix the start of the strip to the ring. Hold the mold with clips or tape so it cannot turn, and then it is just a matter of winding all the way down.

I could not get long bamboo, so I had real trouble with this one. It is probably the way lantern makers more often use, though, so searching YouTube should turn up plenty to learn from.

## Pasting the washi {#pasting}

Once the bamboo is wound, the washi goes on.

There are many kinds of washi, but whatever you can get should be fine. I use the cheap shoji paper.

![Shoji paper](notes/washi-sheets.jpg)

The traditional way seems to be to paste the washi on and then trim the excess with a razor. That can be hard depending on the paper, so the washi panels can be generated as a template too, to cut before you paste. They are worked out from the body's own shape, but hold the template itself against the body to check the fit before you cut any washi. The top and bottom are sized to fold over the rings, and that is worth adjusting as well.

![Cut to the washi template](notes/washi-panels.jpg)

To paste: brush the paste onto the bamboo, then lay the washi over it. Spraying the washi with water softens it and makes it easier to lay. If it is still stiff, crumple it into a ball and open it out again — it comes out softer still.

![The paste I use: wallpaper paste and wood glue](notes/paste.jpg)

With the washi in place, stroke it gently with a shoe-polish brush and it takes to the bamboo cleanly. Some washi is stiff; if yours is, try something thinner.

If you pasted without the template, trim the excess with a razor, following the line of the groove the bamboo sits in.

Work round leaving a gap between each panel — it comes out neater. The second time round you fill those gaps in. The smaller the overlap between panels, the better it looks lit.

![Pasted with a gap between each panel. The second round fills them in](notes/washi-alternating.jpg)

## Drying, and pulling the mold {#unmolding}

Once pasted, leave it to dry. A cardboard mold has the washi sticking to the mold as well, so start taking it apart once it has dried somewhat.

Take off the round parts at each end, then work the mold out of the body. As gently as you can, and out through the larger opening.

![Drawing the ribs out through the opening](notes/pulling-the-mold.jpg)

The washi at the rings has sometimes come loose by the time the mold is out, so I clip it back down. Once that dries, the body is done.

![Clipping the loosened washi back down](notes/clipped-at-the-ring.jpg)

Real lanterns get creases so they can be collapsed. You can do that or not. If you do, something like a spatula makes the work easier.

## Fitting the light {#lighting}

The legs and the lamp socket have their own note: [Legs and how to mount the socket](note-sockets).

For a pendant light there is a simpler way as well: bend a piece of wire that catches on the top opening, and just hook the socket onto it.

![The finished lamp](notes/finished.jpg)
